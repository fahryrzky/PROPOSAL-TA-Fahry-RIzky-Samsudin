# Layer 2 — Structure and Framing (On Demand)

These are the distinctive rhetorical moves that give the corpus its identity: the Fact/Fiction scaffold, the contrarian-pivot opener, the cute title, the discursive footnote, the humble close. They are **powerful but optional**. The user chose "Moderate" voice strength, which means: deploy these selectively, where the material earns them — never as a costume dragged onto every paper.

**The guiding question for every Layer 2 move:** *does this paper actually do the thing the move implies?* A Fact/Fiction scaffold on a paper that isn't corrective is dishonest; a cute title on a risk-management paper is tone-deaf. When the answer is no, skip the move and rely on Layer 1 alone.

For verbatim sentences to pattern-match, see [exemplars.md](exemplars.md).

## 1. The hook — concrete + immediately problematized

Open with something specific — a striking number, a popular narrative, a prior paper — and *immediately* signal you will complicate it. Do not open with philosophy, literature review, or "the importance of X."

**The contrarian-pivot opener (signature move):**
> "Given its pedigree, you would be forgiven for thinking that the size factor is one of the strongest and most robust anomalies in finance. In fact, it is one of the weakest." — Size

**Three reliable hook patterns:**

| Pattern | When to use | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Striking-number hook** | You have one headline statistic | "Few papers focus solely on subjects as seemingly innocuous as… timely updates of price… But rarely is so innocuous a choice worth between 305 and 378 basis points annually…" — HML |
| **Popular-narrative hook** | You're overturning a belief | "The value premium… experienced very poor performance during the 2010s. A popular hypothesis for value's recent travails relates to the interest rate environment." — Rates |
| **Series-nod hook** | Part of a sequence of papers | "After confronting the myths surrounding momentum investing and value investing, we realized two things…" — Size |

**When NOT to use the contrarian pivot:** if the paper is genuinely confirmatory or descriptive (not overturning anything), a pivot opener is fake drama. Open instead with the striking number or the plain research question.

## 2. The Fact/Fiction scaffold — the brand, used honestly

The most recognizable structural move in the corpus: enumerate a numbered ledger of facts and fictions up front, then devote one body section to each, with headers explicitly tagged `FACT:` / `FICTION:` (or `(FACT)` / `(FICTION)`).

> "This article presents five facts and dispels five fictions about low-risk investing." — Low-Risk *(abstract)*
> "We proceed to tackle the following 10 facts and fictions associated with factor investing." — Factor

**How to build it:**
1. In the introduction, list the facts and fictions as a numbered ledger (interleaved or grouped).
2. Make each body section a standalone `FACT:` or `FICTION:` statement in a full-sentence, ALL-CAPS header.
3. The authors flag that the split is somewhat arbitrary — you can too: "We could choose to describe something as a 'fact' or 'fiction' at will… We choose the framing we think most intuitive but recognize it is an arbitrary decision." — Factor

**When to use it:** the paper is *genuinely corrective* — it exists to dispel specific misconceptions or assert specific under-appreciated truths. Each item should be a real claim a reader might hold.

**When NOT to use it (this is the important part):**
- The paper is a methods/estimation piece (use HML's question-form headers instead).
- The paper is descriptive or survey-style.
- You would have to invent or stretch a "fiction" to fill the scaffold. **Never invent a fiction to fit the frame** — that fabricates a debate the field isn't having, and violates the hard rule against inventing claims.
- The user is writing for an outlet whose house style doesn't use it (Financial Analysts Journal uses it less than JPM).

If unsure, default to **plain full-sentence section headers** (see §4) and skip the scaffold. Layer 1 alone produces a perfectly good practitioner piece.

## 3. Rhetorical questions — set up, then answer

Use a question to launch a section, dramatize a puzzle, or set up an immediate answer. The answer should follow within a sentence or two — a question left dangling reads as rhetorical slop.

> "How would you exploit a situation in which safe and risky stocks deliver similar returns?" — Low-Risk *(section opener)*
> "Should we stop considering the facts and fictions related to value investing…? We argue no." — Value *(question + immediate answer)*

**Rule of thumb:** one rhetorical question per major section, maximum. If you find yourself writing three in a row, rewrite as declaratives.

## 4. Section headers — full sentences or questions, not bare nouns

Even without the Fact/Fiction scaffold, headers should be **claims or questions**, not topic labels. A header that announces the verdict primes the reader for the argument.

| Bare noun (strip) | Full-sentence / question header (reach for) |
|---|---|
| "Data and Methodology" | "DATA, METHODOLOGY, AND TERMINOLOGY" |
| "The B/P Measure" | "What Proxies Best for the True Unobservable B/P?" |
| "Robustness" | "EMPIRICAL RELATIONSHIPS WITH INTEREST RATE LEVELS AND CONTEMPORANEOUS CHANGES" |
| "Case Study" | "A RECENT CASE STUDY: THE CORONAVIRUS PANDEMIC AND AFTERMATH" |

The corpus uses ALL-CAPS for major section headers (journal house style). If the user's target outlet or template uses sentence case, match that — the principle (claim-headers, not noun-headers) matters more than the casing.

## 5. Titles — cute/literary core + serious or questioning subtitle

The title does work: a memorable lead (idiom, triad, pun, or question) plus a subtitle that tells the reader the actual topic.

| Title | Pattern |
|---|---|
| "Fact, Fiction, and Value Investing" | Series triad |
| "The Devil in HML's Details" | Idiom / pun |
| "Value and Interest Rates: Are Rates to Blame for Value's Torments?" | Serious topic + wry question subtitle |

**When NOT to be cute:** a paper on risk management, drawdowns, or methodology should use a straight descriptive title — wit in a title about other people's losses is tone-deaf. "Timely Price Updates in Book-to-Market Ratios" would have been a fine title; "The Devil in HML's Details" works because the topic (a measurement tweak worth 300 bps) is light enough to carry the pun.

**Offer titles as options.** When drafting, give the user 2–3 title variants spanning the cute-to-straight spectrum and let them choose.

## 6. Discursive footnotes — asides, not just citations

Numbered footnotes in the corpus carry wit, biography, data provenance, and methodological asides — not just references. They are where the author's personality lives when the main text stays professional.

> On Fischer Black: Wells Fargo rejected low-risk investing, making him so upset that he "stomped out… the nearly unique instance when Fischer lost his cool." The meeting was later called **"the day alpha died."** — Low-Risk *(footnote)*
> "Cigar Aficionado published an article in 1994 with the following incredulous quote… Of course, there is a good chance they were joking. We certainly hope so…" — Value *(footnote)*

**Use footnotes for:** data-source provenance ("data are from Ken French's website"), biographical or historical asides, self-aware methodological caveats, and comic tangents that would break the main argument's flow. Keep the main text lean; push the digression down.

**Do not** force wit into a footnote that doesn't have a real aside to carry. An empty discursive footnote is worse than none.

## 7. Conclusions — land on something, then invite challenge

End on a calibrated, epigrammatic note — not a data summary. The corpus conclusions do three things: (1) deliver the "bottom line," (2) acknowledge limits or credit prior work, (3) explicitly invite the reader to overturn them.

> "The bottom line is that the standard approach to value was a reasonable, conservative choice that has served the field well. **But it is not the best possible choice.**" — HML
> "As always, we welcome new challenges and debates and especially new evidence and ideas, even if they run counter to some of our own views. **We wish to learn.**" — Factor

**Three reliable close patterns:**

| Pattern | When to use | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Logical zinger** | The argument reduces to a contradiction | "it cannot be both steep and flat." — Low-Risk |
| **Philosophical reframing** | The negative result is itself the insight | "may be precisely why these factors are risky and therefore why they provide long-term positive return premiums…" — Rates |
| **Humble invitation** | You want to signal openness | "we welcome it and wish to understand it." — Value |

**Do not** end with a generic "future research could extend…" or a recapitulated abstract. The reader just read the paper; give them the verdict and the implication.

---

## Layer 2 deployment checklist

Before adding any Layer 2 move, ask:
- [ ] Is this paper actually corrective? (If no → skip Fact/Fiction scaffold and contrarian pivot.)
- [ ] Is the topic light enough for wit / a cute title? (If serious → straight title, dry register.)
- [ ] Does each rhetorical question get answered within two sentences?
- [ ] Does every "fiction" I assert correspond to a real claim someone holds? (If invented → cut it.)
- [ ] Does the footnote I'm adding carry a real aside? (If empty → cut it.)
- [ ] Does the conclusion land on a verdict, not a summary?
