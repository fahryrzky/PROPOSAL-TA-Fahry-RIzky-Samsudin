# Layer 1 — Voice and Register (Always On)

These are the durable house-style traits that **every** practitioner-journal piece shares, regardless of topic. Apply them to every transform, whether the user asked for "moderate" or "strong." They are what make academic prose stop sounding like a *Journal of Finance* article and start sounding like *Financial Analysts Journal* / *Journal of Portfolio Management*.

This layer **assumes** the baseline clarity rules from `paper-taste` and `econ-write` (active voice by default, one idea per sentence, precision over abstraction, no stacked hedges). It does not repeat them. It layers the practitioner *voice* on top.

For verbatim sentences to pattern-match, see [exemplars.md](exemplars.md).

## 1. Persona: an opinionated expert, not a neutral technician

The single biggest tell that separates academic prose from practitioner prose is the **authorial presence**. A *Journal of Finance* paper hides the author behind passive voice and "the literature." A practitioner piece tells you what the author *believes*.

**Take a stand. Use "we."** Narrate as a collective ("we find," "we argue," "we believe," "in our view"). Address the reader directly as "you." Insert judgment openly — the reader is paying for your opinion, not your agnosticism.

| Academic (strip) | Practitioner (reach for) |
|---|---|
| "It is found that the size effect is weak." | "**We find** that the size effect is weak." |
| "It can be shown that value works best with other factors." | "**We believe** value works best with other factors." |
| "Investors may consider…" | "**You** could have earned the same premium at a third lower risk." |
| "This paper examines…" | "**Our** aim is to clarify truths and address misconceptions." |

The authors even disclose their own biases and second-guessing — that candor *builds* credibility, it doesn't undercut it:
> "Full disclosure: We intended to write this article in 2019, but being known proponents of factors, we balked at the risk of looking like we were just trying to excuse recent events." — **Factor**

**Do not** swing to the opposite extreme: never use "I" in a multi-author paper, never rant, never make the piece about the author rather than the evidence. The persona is *measured partisan*, not columnist.

## 2. Sentence rhythm: long + short, deliberately

Long multi-clause sentences carry the technical detail; then a very short one delivers the verdict. The short sentence is the beat the reader remembers.

> "Given its pedigree, you would be forgiven for thinking that the size factor is one of the strongest and most robust anomalies in finance. **In fact, it is one of the weakest.**" — Size

> "We believe that this was entirely reasonable, particularly in the early days of the literature… **Now, however, it is suboptimal.**" — HML

**Mechanics:**
- After any sentence longer than ~25 words, look for the one-line verdict hiding inside it and give it its own sentence. ("Far from it." / "It is one of the weakest." / "This is not a coincidence.")
- Em-dashes and parentheticals are the connective tissue — use them to tuck qualifications and asides *inside* a sentence rather than spinning up a new one.
- Parallelism is a signature move: "If the CAPM is dead, then BAB is alive. If BAB dies, then the CAPM comes alive."

**Do not** make every sentence the same length. A paragraph of five uniform 20-word sentences reads like a textbook; the practitioner voice needs the short punchy beat.

## 3. Paragraph shape: topic sentence → evidence → punchline

Every paragraph opens with the claim, earns it in the middle, and closes on something — a verdict, a rhetorical question, a quip, or a logical zinger. Paragraphs do **not** trail off into hedged summary.

| Element | Job | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Topic sentence** | State the claim up front | "Size has never been a very strong effect." |
| **Middle** | Evidence, qualification, the counter | stats, exhibits, the concession |
| **Punchline close** | Land it — verdict, question, zinger | "…it is not. It is one of the weakest." |

Typical length: 4–7 sentences. If a paragraph has no punchline, either write one or cut the paragraph — it probably isn't carrying its weight.

**Common punchline shapes:**
- *One-line verdict:* "As it is, the choice is anything but innocuous."
- *Logical contradiction:* "it cannot be both steep and flat."
- *Quip:* "Being Warren Buffett is nice work if you can get it, certainly after-the-fact."
- *Forward-looking question:* "This raises a question: Should academics and investors be using a size-based factor?"

## 4. The concession tic

Before swatting a counter-argument, **grant it**. This is what makes the voice read as measured rather than strident — and it pre-empts the reviewer's objection. Reach for "Of course," "It is true that," "In fairness," "Admittedly," "To be sure."

> "It is true that BAB works across industries, **but** it also works reliably within industries." — Low-Risk

> "We believe that this was entirely reasonable, particularly in the early days of the literature… **Now, however**, it is suboptimal." — HML *(concede the legacy method, then replace it)*

> "We cannot rule out the monetary policy environment having played some role… Rather, the right explanation is often the simplest one…" — Rates

**Do not** overdo it — one concession per counter-argument, not three stacked "of courses." And never concede something you then refuse to address; the concession must be followed by the pivot.

## 5. The restatement tic

State the idea formally, then immediately restate it in plain English. The authors explain every important idea **twice** — once for the specialist, once for everyone else. This is non-negotiable for a practitioner audience.

> "We are 'explaining' low risk using low risk! **In other words,** low-risk stocks perform well because low-risk stocks perform well." — Low-Risk

> "A high B/P means a stock is cheap **(or high risk, to efficient market fans)** and has a high expected return." — HML *(parenthetical gloss)*

**Reach for:** "In other words," "Put differently," "That is," "Stated another way," "To put it simply." After a number, add its plain meaning: "a t-statistic of 1.21 — not statistically significant."

## 6. The two registers — dial to the topic

The corpus contains two recognizable registers. Most papers mix them: dry for the empirics, witty for the framing. Dial the mix to the topic **and** to the author's own preference. When in doubt, default to **dry-measured-verdict** — it is the safer baseline and the user chose "Moderate."

### Dry-measured-verdict (default)
- **Vibe:** authoritative, calm, a little sorrowful; "this emperor has no clothes."
- **Tell-tale signs:** semicolons, em-dash qualifications, logical-zinger closes, very few exclamation marks, "the bottom line is…"
- **Papers:** Size, HML's Details, Value & Interest Rates, Low-Risk.
- **Use when:** the topic is serious (drawdowns, methodology, risk), the evidence is mixed, or the author's natural voice is restrained.

### Witty-extrovert (deploy selectively)
- **Vibe:** conversational, contrarian, name-dropping, comic.
- **Tell-tale signs:** exclamation marks, named names (Buffett, George Burns, Aristotle), parenthetical asides, self-disclosure, occasional slang ("pretty darn"), literary title play.
- **Papers:** Value Investing (2015), Factor Investing (2023).
- **Use when:** the topic is corrective/myth-busting, the audience is broad, or the author wants personality. **Never** force it onto a methodology or risk-management paper — wit about other people's losses reads as tone-deaf.

### How to choose
If the user's draft is already dry and restrained, keep them in the dry register and just add the practitioner scaffolding (opinion-forward "we," prose-narrated stats, "so what"). If they explicitly ask to "make it punchy" or are writing a contrarian take, dial up the witty register. When uncertain, ask.

## 7. Hedging — calibrated, not stacked

The user's `paper-taste` skill already bans stacked hedges. The practitioner voice adds one nuance: a **little** hedging signals credibility here, because the authors are often overturning popular beliefs and want to stay defensible. Use at most one hedge per claim, and pick the strongest the evidence supports.

| Too strong (overclaim) | Stacked (strip) | Calibrated (reach for) |
|---|---|---|
| "proves" | "may perhaps potentially suggest" | "**suggests**" / "**is consistent with**" |
| "demonstrates" | "could possibly indicate" | "**indicates**" / "the evidence **points to**" |

The corpus's favorite calibrated verbs: "suggests," "is consistent with," "appears to," "seems to," "we find," "the evidence indicates." Pair them with concrete numbers so the hedge reads as precision, not timidity.

## Quick self-check (Layer 1 applied?)

- [ ] Does the prose use "we" and take a stand, not hide behind passive voice?
- [ ] Does at least some paragraph end on a verdict, question, or zinger — not a trailing hedge?
- [ ] Are long sentences broken by at least one short punchy one?
- [ ] Is every key idea restated in plain English somewhere nearby?
- [ ] Does every statistic carry its verdict (significant / not / economically meaningful) inline?
- [ ] Does every finding get a one-line "so what" for a portfolio manager?
- [ ] Is the register consistent (dry default) and not lurching into unearned wit?
