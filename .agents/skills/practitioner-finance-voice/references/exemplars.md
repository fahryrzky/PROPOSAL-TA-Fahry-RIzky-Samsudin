# Exemplar Library — Practitioner-Journal Voice

Verbatim sentences from the corpus, keyed by rhetorical move. Use these to **pattern-match real prose**, not to paraphrase abstract rules. When a move fits, reach for one of these cadences and adapt it to the user's actual content.

**Source key** (all papers are *The Journal of Portfolio Management*):

## 1. Contrarian-pivot openers & hooks

Open with something concrete — a popular belief, a prior paper, a striking number — and *immediately* signal you will complicate it. The signature move: "You'd be forgiven for thinking X… In fact, not-X."

- "Given its pedigree, you would be forgiven for thinking that the size factor is one of the strongest and most robust anomalies in finance. In fact, it is one of the weakest." — **Size**
- "Few papers focus solely on subjects as seemingly innocuous as timely, frequent updates of price (P) in calculations of B/P. But rarely is so innocuous a choice worth between 305 and 378 basis points annually of statistically significant alpha…" — **HML**
- "The value premium… experienced very poor performance during the 2010s. A popular hypothesis for value's recent travails relates to the interest rate environment." — **Rates** *(then spends the paper dismantling it)*
- "Factor investing has been around for a long time and an immense literature in academia and practice is devoted to it. Yet, much confusion about it remains." — **Factor**
- "After confronting the myths surrounding momentum investing and value investing, we realized two things: 1) We had passed over the first anomaly discovered in academic finance… —size, and 2) despite its longevity… there is still much confusion and debate surrounding the size anomaly." — **Size** *(series-nod hook)*

**Anti-example to avoid:** never open with "Financial economists have long wondered whether…" or "Given the importance of X…" — that is academic throat-clearing.

## 2. Topic-sentence-first paragraph openings

Lead with the claim. The rest of the paragraph earns it.

- "Size has never been a very strong effect." — **Size**
- "We find, however, that the relationship between value factor returns and the interest rate environment is not very robust." — **Rates**
- "Low-risk strategies have delivered large risk-adjusted returns, and this strong risk-adjusted performance has persisted for nearly a century both in-sample and out-of-sample (OOS)." — **Low-Risk**
- "Clearly, value does not work best alone. Far from it." — **Value**

## 3. Short punchy sentences (rhythm-breakers) & one-line verdicts

After a long technical sentence, drop a short one. This is the heartbeat of the register.

- "In fact, it is one of the weakest." — **Size**
- "Now, however, it is suboptimal." — **HML**
- "Far from it." — **Value**
- "All is good so far." — **Value**
- "Now, on to the main event." — **Value** *(transition as one-liner)*
- "This is not a coincidence, but the outcome of solid science." — **Factor**
- "We wish to learn." — **Factor** *(closing one-liner)*

## 4. Punchline paragraph endings & logical zingers

Paragraphs don't trail off — they land on a verdict, a question, or a contradiction.

- "…there is little debate about whether size is one of the strongest anomalies—it is not. It is one of the weakest." — **Size**
- "…to say the reverse—that the low-risk premium is weaker than other factor premiums—is clearly a fiction." — **Low-Risk**
- "If the CAPM is dead, then BAB is alive. If BAB dies, then the CAPM comes alive… it cannot be both steep and flat." — **Low-Risk** *(reductio close)*
- "As it is, the choice is anything but innocuous." — **HML** *(deliberate callback to the opening line)*
- "Being Warren Buffett is nice work if you can get it, certainly after-the-fact." — **Value** *(quip close)*
- "It is exactly value. … It is time for us all to acknowledge that." — **Value**

## 5. Concession moves

Grant the opposing point *before* you restate your own. This is what makes the voice read as measured rather than strident. Reach for "Of course," "It is true that," "In fairness," "Admittedly."

- "Although we do not completely deny the existence of a size effect, and we certainly do not advocate actively betting against or shorting it, we also do not believe that size on its own is a key factor…" — **Size**
- "We believe that this was entirely reasonable, particularly in the early days of the literature… Now, however, it is suboptimal." — **HML** *(concede the legacy, then replace it)*
- "It is true that BAB works across industries, but it also works reliably within industries." — **Low-Risk**
- "In fairness, Fama and French (2016) do not make this blatant contradiction…" — **Low-Risk**
- "Of course, these explanations are not mutually exclusive." — **Factor**
- "We cannot rule out the monetary policy environment having played some role in the market's repricing of growth versus value companies in recent years." — **Rates**
- "Of course, good science (and good investing) should be wary of inferring a causal relationship from a single observation of two market phenomena that happen to coincide." — **Rates**

## 6. Restatement moves

State the idea formally, then immediately restate it in plain English. The authors explain every important idea *twice*.

- "We are 'explaining' low risk using low risk! In other words, low-risk stocks perform well because low-risk stocks perform well." — **Low-Risk**
- "A high B/P means a stock is cheap (or high risk, to efficient market fans) and has a high expected return." — **HML** *(parenthetical plain-English gloss)*
- "Rather, the right explanation is often the simplest one (albeit less satisfying perhaps)—large drawdowns are simply an unwanted feature of factor premiums such as value…" — **Rates**
- "the standard t-statistic threshold of 2.0… gives the researcher roughly 95% confidence that the result is not a false discovery." — **Factor** *(number → plain meaning)*

**Reach for:** "In other words," "Put differently," "That is," "To put it simply."

## 7. Rhetorical questions

Used to launch a section, set up a rebuttal, or dramatize a puzzle. Never rhetorical-for-its-own-sake.

- "How would you exploit a situation in which safe and risky stocks deliver similar returns?" — **Low-Risk** *(section opener)*
- "Does the fallen price make this more likely, less likely, or have no effect on whether this should be considered a value stock?" — **HML**
- "Should we stop considering the facts and fictions related to value investing and concentrate instead on just the other four factors? … We argue no." — **Value** *(question + immediate answer)*
- "This raises a question: Should academics and investors be using a size-based factor?" — **Size**

## 8. Vivid analogies, aphorisms & personification

Reach outside finance for intuition. Use sparingly — one memorable analogy per section, not per paragraph.

- "Warren Buffett is to value investing what George Burns, the comedian who lived to 99 years of age while smoking 10 to 15 cigars a day for 70-plus years, is to the health effects of smoking." — **Value**
- "As the saying at the University of Chicago goes, 'the plural of anecdote is not data.'" — **Value**
- "if one thinks of citations as the currency of the academic market, 'academic market prices' of these factor discoveries are perfectly aligned with their veracity and reliability." — **Factor**
- "value was there to save the day" / "univariate momentum was a complete dud" / "UMD is resurrected" — **HML** *(personification)*
- "in sports betting, the long-shot bias is a clear example of the low-risk effect." — **Low-Risk** *(cross-domain)*

## 9. Prose-narrated statistics

Never dump a number. Deliver the statistic, the benchmark, the t-stat, *and* the verdict in a single breath.

- "SMB has a 1.9% annualized mean return with almost 10% annual volatility, translating into a 0.19 Sharpe ratio. The mean return of SMB over the 1936 to 1975 period, however, is not statistically significant, with a t-statistic of only 1.21." — **Size**
- "the standard HML approach subtracts −58 basis points (bps) annually (which is statistically insignificant). Our more timely HML factor adds 143 bps (which is statistically significant)…" — **HML** *(contrast pair with verdicts)*
- "a very significant intercept of 305 bps a year with a +5.92 t-statistic." — **HML**
- "Such a portfolio has a backtested Sharpe ratio of 1.1 with a t-statistic of 10.8, as documented by Ilmanen et al. (2021) using data from 1926 to 2020." — **Factor**
- "The t-statistics on the interest rate-level variables are all well below 2, indicating the associated coefficients… are indistinguishable from zero." — **Rates** *(stat → plain-English meaning)*

**Exhibit narration:** "Simple eyeballing of Exhibit 6 confirms this claim." / "Exhibit 2 clearly shows the historical success of low-risk strategies." — **Low-Risk**

## 10. Practitioner "so what" lines

Every finding gets translated into what it means for a portfolio manager — timing, Sharpe, after-cost, drawdown behavior.

- "If a strategy works only because it has a bigger market beta, then a more efficient and reliable way to capture those returns is simply to allocate more to the market factor itself." — **Size**
- "investors could have earned the same equity premium as a value-weighted market index with roughly a third lower risk (a beta of 0.67 instead of 1.0 or a volatility of 10% rather than 15%)." — **Low-Risk**
- "a value timing strategy based on interest rate signals is likely to yield poor out-of-sample performance." — **Rates**
- "Being able to stick with short-term drawdowns, in order to reap the long-term returns associated with a factor, requires a level of faith and confidence." — **Factor**
- "Whether products are active or passive is irrelevant, so long as they add value to the portfolio after cost." — **Value**

## 11. Inline definitions & plain-English unpacking

Define jargon the moment it appears, in apposition. Never make the reader leave the page.

- "often referred to as HML (high minus low)" — **HML**
- "an anomaly (i.e., a trading opportunity)" — **Low-Risk**
- "Sharpe ratio (SR, excess return divided by volatility)" — **Low-Risk**
- "Value is the phenomenon in which securities that appear cheap, on average, outperform securities that appear to be expensive." — **Value**
- "Before proceeding, it is useful to define factor investing. We define it as a systematic tilting toward a style/theme (and away from its polar opposite) implemented across a diversified set of assets." — **Factor**
- "Imagine you're standing at Dec 31, 2000, and you want to form a value portfolio based on B/P." — **HML** *(scenario-setting)*

**Worked dollar example:** "buying $2 worth of safe stocks and shorting $0.67 worth of risky ones, creating an expected excess return of 2 × 10% − 0.67 × 12% = 12%." — **Low-Risk**

## 12. Discursive / witty footnotes & historical anecdote

Footnotes carry asides, biography, data provenance, and comic tangents — not just citations.

- "Cigar Aficionado published an article in 1994 with the following incredulous quote: 'Comedian George Burns is not only a living legend, he's living proof that smoking between 10 and 15 cigars a day for 70 years contributes to one's longevity.'… Of course, there is a good chance they were joking. We certainly hope so…" — **Value** *(footnote)*
- On Fischer Black: Wells Fargo rejected low-risk investing, making him so upset that he "stomped out, an event memorable as the nearly unique instance when Fischer lost his cool." The meeting was later referred to as **"the day alpha died."** — **Low-Risk** *(footnote)*
- "Not Bad—still an anomaly and counts as CAPM alpha." — **Low-Risk** *(dry footnote aside)*
- "We could choose to describe something as a 'fact' or 'fiction' at will… We choose the framing we think most intuitive but recognize it is an arbitrary decision." — **Factor** *(self-aware methodological footnote)*

## 13. "Bottom line" conclusions & humble/inviting endings

End on a calibrated, epigrammatic note — not a data summary. Acknowledge limits; credit prior work; invite challenge.

- "The bottom line is that the standard approach to value was a reasonable, conservative choice that has served the field well. But it is not the best possible choice." — **HML**
- "The bottom line is that, after addressing the facts and fictions of the size effect, we find neither strong empirical evidence nor robust theoretical support for a prominent size premium." — **Size**
- "The paucity of evidence that these drawdowns can reliably be explained or predicted by observable variables… may be precisely why these factors are risky and therefore why they provide long-term positive return premiums that are not easily arbitraged away." — **Rates** *(philosophical close)*
- "As always, if someone discovers something challenging or enlightening versus what we have shown, we welcome it and wish to understand it." — **Value**
- "As always, we welcome new challenges and debates and especially new evidence and ideas, even if they run counter to some of our own views. We wish to learn." — **Factor**

## 14. Cute / literary titles & section headers

Title = idiom or triad + serious/questioning subtitle. Section headers are full sentences or questions, not bare nouns.

**Titles:**
- "Fact, Fiction, and the Size Effect" / "Fact, Fiction, and Value Investing" / "Fact, Fiction, and Factor Investing" — **series template**
- "The Devil in HML's Details" — **HML** *(idiom/pun)*
- "Value and Interest Rates: Are Rates to Blame for Value's Torments?" — **Rates** *(serious + wry question subtitle)*

**Section headers (full-sentence claims, tagged FACT/FICTION):**
- "FICTION: THE SIZE EFFECT IS ONE OF THE STRONGEST DOCUMENTED ANOMALIES/FACTORS" — **Size**
- "FACT: 'FUNDAMENTAL INDEXING' IS ONLY SYSTEMATIC VALUE INVESTING" — **Value**
- "LOW-RISK ASSETS OUTPERFORM HIGH-RISK ASSETS ON A RISK-ADJUSTED BASIS (FACT)" — **Low-Risk**
- "#1. Fiction: Factor Investing Is Based on Data-Mined Factors with No Good Economic Story" — **Factor**
- "What Proxies Best for the True Unobservable B/P?" — **HML** *(question form)*
- "A RECENT CASE STUDY: THE CORONAVIRUS PANDEMIC AND AFTERMATH" — **Rates**

## 15. Self-disclosure & confessional asides

The authors tell you what they believe, what they once balked at, even where they were wrong. This is the opposite of the neutral technician.

- "Full disclosure: We intended to write this article in 2019, but being known proponents of factors, we balked at the risk of looking like we were just trying to excuse recent events." — **Factor**
- "we consider ourselves, and have for many years, among value investing's strongest proponents" — **Value**
- "Quite frankly, we find the debate between active and passive management not that useful." — **Value**
- "some practitioners who shall remain nameless (many, including us), have used significant discounts to back tests in forming expectations…" — **Factor** *(self-including critique)*
- "We asked ourselves why a value portfolio based on more current prices does so much better when combined with momentum… The short answer is that…" — **HML** *(narrating the inquiry)*

### The two registers, at a glance

| Register | Example papers | Tell-tale signs |
|----------|----------------|-----------------|
| **Dry-measured-verdict** | Size, HML, Rates, Low-Risk | Semicolons, em-dash qualifications, logical-zinger closes, few exclamation marks, "the bottom line is…" |
| **Witty-extrovert** | Value, Factor | Exclamation marks, named names (Buffett, George Burns, Aristotle), parenthetical asides, self-disclosure, slang ("pretty darn," "waaaaay!"), literary title play |

Most papers mix the two — dry for the empirics, witty for the framing. Dial the mix to the topic and to the author's own voice.
