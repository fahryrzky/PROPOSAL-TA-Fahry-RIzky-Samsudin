# Reviewer Expectations for Marketing Journals

This reference covers what reviewers look for at each major marketing journal, common criticisms in review reports, how to preempt and address them, and effective rebuttal strategies. Marketing reviewers are distinct in their emphasis on **substantive relevance**, **identification credibility**, and **managerial actionability**.

---

## 1. Universal Expectations (All Marketing Journals)

Every reviewer at every marketing journal evaluates papers on these dimensions:

| Dimension | Weight | Key Question |
|-----------|--------|-------------|
| **Substantive Contribution** | 30% | Does this paper address a real marketing problem? Is the contribution non-obvious? |
| **Identification Credibility** | 25% | Are the causal claims credible? Is the source of identification convincing? |
| **Managerial Relevance** | 20% | What should a Chief Marketing Officer or brand manager DO differently? |
| **Methodological Rigor** | 15% | Are the methods correctly applied? Are there diagnostic tests? |
| **Positioning & Writing** | 10% | Is the paper well-situated in the literature? Is it clearly written? |

**The Marketing Reviewer's Mindset**:

Marketing reviewers are trained to ask: "If I were the CMO of a Fortune 500 company, would I change my marketing budget allocation because of this paper?" Unlike MS&E reviewers who may accept methodological contributions, marketing reviewers almost always demand a **substantive marketing insight**.

## 2. Journal-Specific Reviewer Priorities

### Marketing Science (MktSci)

**Publisher**: INFORMS | **Editorial Focus**: Quantitative, model-driven marketing research

**What MktSci Reviewers REALLY Look For**:

1. **The substantive marketing story**: Can you explain your paper's contribution without equations in 2-3 sentences?
2. **The structural model**: Is it appropriate for the marketing problem? Does it capture the key trade-offs?
3. **Novel data**: Marketing Science rarely publishes papers that use only publicly available data. Novel, proprietary data is expected.
4. **The counterfactual**: Does the paper simulate a policy change that a real marketing manager would consider? (Merger, price change, product introduction, advertising reallocation)
5. **The model-data connection**: Every parameter in the model should be connected to something in the data. "Estimate everything, assume nothing."

**Red Flags for MktSci Reviewers**:
- "The structural model is a standard BLP with no innovation"
- "The data is not novel—this is the same Nielsen/IRI scanner data used in 50 previous papers"
- "The counterfactual is incremental and does not change managerial practice"
- "The identification is not credible—where does the exogenous variation come from?"
- "The paper could be a JMR paper" (i.e., identification is strong but the model is not structural)

**MktSci Review Form Structure**:
```
1. Summary assessment (1 paragraph)
2. Contribution to marketing science (major concern #1)
3. Model specification and identification (major concern #2)
4. Estimation and empirical implementation (major concern #3)
5. Counterfactual analysis (major concern #4)
6. Substantive marketing implications (major concern #5)
7. Minor comments
```

**Typical MktSci Decision Rates**: ~8-10% acceptance rate. Desk rejection: ~40-50%.

### Journal of Marketing Research (JMR)

**Publisher**: AMA | **Editorial Focus**: Empirical rigor in service of marketing practice

**What JMR Reviewers REALLY Look For**:

1. **Identification credibility above all else**: JMR is known as the journal where identification matters most. A weak identification strategy is fatal.
2. **Methodological rigor**: Whether you use experiments, quasi-experiments, or structural models, the method must be applied flawlessly.
3. **Managerial relevance**: The "Implications for Marketing Practice" section is mandatory. It must be specific and actionable.
4. **Robustness**: Expect 8-15 robustness checks. Not 3. Reviewers will ask for MORE.
5. **Transparency**: Pre-registration is encouraged. Data and code should be available. Replication studies are welcome.

**Red Flags for JMR Reviewers**:
- "The instrumental variable is weak (F < 10)"
- "Parallel trends are assumed, not tested"
- "The experiment is underpowered"
- "There are no placebo or falsification tests"
- "The implications section is generic—any marketing paper could claim this"
- "Selection on unobservables is not addressed"

**JMR Review Form Structure**:
```
1. Summary (1 paragraph)
2. Contribution and positioning (major)
3. Identification strategy / experimental design (major, most weight)
4. Empirical analysis and results (major)
5. Robustness checks (major)
6. Implications for marketing practice (major)
7. Writing and presentation (minor)
```

**JMR Specialty**: Field experiments. JMR is the top outlet for marketing field experiments. The experiment must be well-designed, well-powered, and have clear practical implications.

### Journal of Marketing (JM)

**Publisher**: AMA | **Editorial Focus**: Broad marketing strategy and substantive contribution

**What JM Reviewers REALLY Look For**:

1. **The "big idea"**: JM wants conceptual contributions that change how we think about marketing. Pure empirical papers need a strong conceptual framework.
2. **Multi-method validation**: The best JM papers use multiple methods (survey + experiment + secondary data) to triangulate on a phenomenon.
3. **Managerial relevance is paramount**: More than any other journal, JM emphasizes implications for practice. The CMO test is real here.
4. **Strategy orientation**: How does this affect marketing strategy, not just marketing tactics?
5. **Conceptual framework**: A figure showing the conceptual model is expected in the front of the paper.

**Red Flags for JM Reviewers**:
- "Interesting finding, but what's the conceptual contribution?"
- "This is a JMR paper" (strong on method, weak on concept)
- "The implications are tactical, not strategic"
- "Too narrow—this applies only to one industry/context"
- "The conceptual framework is underdeveloped"

**JM Review Form Structure**:
```
1. Summary
2. Conceptual contribution (major)
3. Theoretical framework (major)
4. Empirical approach and findings (major)
5. Managerial implications (major)
6. Generalizability (moderate)
7. Minor comments
```

**JM Distinction**: JM publishes review papers, conceptual papers, and mixed-method empirical papers. Pure behavioral experiments (without strategy implications) go to JCR. Pure structural models go to Marketing Science.

### Journal of Consumer Research (JCR)

**Publisher**: Oxford/JCR | **Editorial Focus**: Consumer behavior and psychology

**What JCR Reviewers REALLY Look For**:

1. **Psychological mechanism**: What is the psychological process? Through what mediator does the IV affect the DV?
2. **Theoretical contribution to consumer behavior theory**: Not just "we show that X affects Y," but WHY does X affect Y?
3. **Process evidence**: Mediation analysis, moderation-of-process, measurement-of-mediation
4. **Multiple experiments**: Typically 4-6 experiments per paper, each building on the last
5. **Convergent evidence**: Lab experiments + field data + behavioral measures

**Red Flags for JCR Reviewers**:
- "This is a JMR paper" (strong on method, weak on theory/process)
- "Only one experiment without process evidence"
- "The theoretical contribution is incremental"
- "Alternative explanations are not ruled out"
- "The manipulation checks are weak"

**JCR Review Form Structure**:
```
1. Summary
2. Theoretical contribution (major)
3. Hypotheses development (major)
4. Study 1: design, results, alternative explanations
5. Study 2-5: each with same evaluation
6. General discussion (integration, theoretical implications)
7. Minor comments
```

**JCR Distinction**: JCR papers are theory-driven. The introduction starts with a theoretical puzzle, not a managerial one. Consumer welfare is the primary lens, not firm profit.

### Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science (JAMS)

**Publisher**: Springer/AMS | **Editorial Focus**: Broad, inclusive, review-friendly

**What JAMS Reviewers Look For**:
1. **Broad contribution**: JAMS values papers with broad implications across marketing subfields
2. **Conceptual + empirical**: Both theory and data are valued
3. **Review papers**: JAMS publishes high-quality review/meta-analysis papers
4. **Methodological diversity**: All methods are welcome if appropriately applied
5. **Timeliness**: Papers addressing current marketing challenges (digital transformation, sustainability, AI)

**Red Flags for JAMS Reviewers**:
- "Too narrow—this is a specialist journal paper"
- "The review is not systematic"
- "The contribution is not differentiated from existing work"

---

### International Journal of Research in Marketing (IJRM)

**Publisher**: Elsevier/EMAC | **Editorial Focus**: European tradition, methodological pluralism

**What IJRM Reviewers Look For**:
1. **International or cross-cultural dimension**: Not required, but valued
2. **Methodological innovation is valued**: New methods well-explained are welcome
3. **Strong special issue culture**: Many papers are solicited for special issues
4. **Replication and meta-analysis**: IJRM values cumulative science
5. **European marketing contexts**: Studies set in European markets are well-received

**Red Flags for IJRM Reviewers**:
- "US-only sample without justification"
- "Method is standard with no innovation or improvement"

---

### Quantitative Marketing and Economics (QME)

**Publisher**: Springer | **Editorial Focus**: Structural IO, rigorous econometrics

**What QME Reviewers Look For**:
1. **Technical rigor**: This is the most technically demanding marketing journal. The bar for econometric correctness is very high.
2. **Structural econometrics**: Almost all QME papers use structural methods (BLP, dynamic structural, game-theoretic structural)
3. **Economic implications**: Does this matter for antitrust, regulation, or competition policy?
4. **Smaller volume, higher bar**: QME publishes fewer papers but expects higher technical quality

**Red Flags for QME Reviewers**:
- "The structural model is not identified" (fatal)
- "The estimation does not account for [endogeneity, selection, dynamics]"
- "The counterfactual is not economically meaningful"

---

### Marketing Letters

**Publisher**: Springer | **Editorial Focus**: Short, sharp empirical contributions

**What Marketing Letters Reviewers Look For**:
1. **Brevity and clarity**: 20 pages. Every word must count.
2. **Clean empirical contribution**: Simple methods, clear identification, crisp results
3. **Replication and extension**: Marketing Letters values papers that replicate and extend prior work
4. **Novel data or context**: Even a simple method can be publishable if applied to an important new context

**Red Flags for Marketing Letters Reviewers**:
- "Too long—this is a full-length journal paper compressed"
- "The contribution is too incremental even for Marketing Letters"
- "The method is not well-executed"

## 3. Common Reviewer Criticisms and How to Preempt Them

### "What is the substantive marketing problem?"

**This is the #1 rejection reason at Marketing Science and JMR.**

**Preemption Strategy**:
1. Open with a real company, real numbers, and a real marketing decision. "In 2023, Coca-Cola allocated $4.2B to marketing across 200+ countries. Country managers face a persistent challenge: how to allocate this budget between global brand-building and local activation..."
2. Make it clear why existing approaches don't solve this problem
3. Show that the answer changes marketing practice, not just academic understanding

### "The identification strategy is not credible"

**The #1 rejection reason at JMR.**

**Preemption Strategy**:
1. Dedicate an entire section to the identification strategy (not buried in Data)
2. Explain the **institutional context** that generates quasi-experimental variation
3. Show first-stage diagnostics (visual + formal test)
4. Provide placebo and falsification tests
5. Explicitly discuss threats to identification and why they are unlikely in this context
6. Compute Oster bounds or equivalent sensitivity analysis

**Section Template**:
```
3. Identification Strategy
  3.1 Institutional Background
      - Describe the policy, rule, or natural experiment
      - Who decides assignment? What drives variation?
      - Why is it plausible that this variation is as-good-as-random?
  3.2 Identification Assumptions
      - State each assumption formally
      - Test what is testable (balance, pre-trends, density)
      - Argue what is untestable (excludability, no manipulation)
  3.3 Diagnostic Evidence
      - Balance table
      - Event study / RD plot / first-stage table
      - Placebo tests
```

### "What should a marketing manager DO differently?"

**The #1 rejection reason at JM and a very common criticism at JMR.**

**Preemption Strategy**:
1. Use the pattern: **"If you are a marketing manager facing [situation], our results suggest you should [action], which we estimate will increase [outcome] by [magnitude]."**
2. Be specific: not "increase sales," but "increase quarterly sales by 4.2% (95% CI: [2.1%, 6.3%])"
3. Connect implications to specific results (cite tables, figures, parameter estimates)
4. Distinguish between: "we find" (result) and "this implies" (interpretation) and "managers should" (recommendation)

### "The contribution is incremental"

**Common at all journals.**

**Preemption Strategy**:
1. Build a **differentiation table**: rows = key papers, columns = dimensions of contribution, checkmarks = what each paper does
2. Your row should add checkmarks in columns that no other paper covers
3. State clearly: "While prior work has studied X (Author A, Author B) and Y (Author C), we are the first to study X and Y jointly, revealing an interaction that neither stream of literature anticipated."
4. Do NOT overclaim. "First" should be used only when it is literally true.

### "The robustness checks are insufficient"

**Common at JMR and Marketing Science.**

**Preemption Strategy**:
Include ALL of the following (and more if applicable):
- Alternative dependent variable construction
- Alternative sample (exclude outliers, different time window, different geography)
- Alternative specification (different fixed effects, different controls, different functional form)
- Alternative estimator (OLS vs. Poisson, fixed effects vs. random effects, different clustering)
- Different bandwidth (RD) or different control group (DID)
- Placebo treatment at a different time, place, or on a different outcome
- Sensitivity to unobservables (Oster, Rosenbaum, Altonji-Elder-Taber)
- Alternative instruments (if IV) or alternative identification strategy entirely
- Split-sample analysis
- Jackknife / leave-one-out

### "The paper does not fit this journal"

**Desk rejection reason. Preempt by choosing the right journal.**

If you get this in a review, the editor already decided. The best preemption is correct journal selection before submission.

## 4. Department Editor Expectations

### Marketing Science Department Editors

**Role**: Handle 20-30 papers per year. Make desk reject decisions (~50% of submissions). Assign reviewers. Make final publication decisions.

**What They Look For in Initial Screening**:
1. Does the paper have a substantive marketing problem?
2. Does it use a structural model or rigorous quantitative method?
3. Is the data novel?
4. Does the abstract promise a specific, non-obvious insight?
5. Is the paper well-written enough to send to reviewers?

**Desk Rejection Triggers**:
- Pure empirical paper without a structural model
- Standard scanner data application without novelty
- Abstract that reads like "We apply [method] to [data] and find [unsurprising result]"
- Over-length (significantly over 35 pages)
- No clear marketing motivation in first 2 pages

### JMR Editors

**Role**: Similar volume. Desk reject ~40-50%.

**Desk Rejection Triggers**:
- Weak identification strategy
- No substantive marketing problem
- Underpowered experiment
- Paper too narrow (one product category with no generalizable insight)
- Paper that could have been submitted 10 years ago

### JM Editors

**Role**: Lower submission volume; higher desk acceptance rate. But conceptual bar is very high.

**Desk Rejection Triggers**:
- No conceptual framework
- Tactic-oriented, not strategy-oriented
- Empirical-only without theoretical contribution

## 5. Rebuttal Strategies for Marketing Papers

### Structure of a Marketing Rebuttal Letter

```
Dear Editor [Name],

We sincerely thank you and the reviewers for the careful reading of our
manuscript and the constructive, detailed feedback. We have substantially
revised the manuscript to address all comments. Our revisions are summarized
below, with detailed point-by-point responses following.

Summary of Major Revisions:
1. [Major change #1] — see pages X-Y
2. [Major change #2] — see pages X-Y
3. [Major change #3] — see pages X-Y

We believe these revisions have significantly strengthened the paper's
contribution, identification credibility, and managerial relevance. We
hope the revised manuscript is now suitable for publication in [Journal].

Sincerely,
The Authors
```

### Rebuttal Principles for Marketing Journals

1. **Address the identification concern first**: If a reviewer questions identification, respond with additional evidence, not argument. Run the placebo. Add the sensitivity analysis. Show the alternative specification.

2. **Add the robustness they ask for**: Marketing reviewers expect you to add the robustness checks they request. If you cannot, explain precisely why (data limitation, methodological impossibility) and propose what you CAN do.

3. **Substantive implications must be strengthened**: If a reviewer says implications are weak, add specific numbers, connect to real companies, or add a paragraph about implementation challenges.

4. **Never argue about contribution**: If a reviewer says the contribution is incremental, do not argue. Instead, clarify what is new about your paper, add a differentiation table, or identify additional contributions you hadn't emphasized.

5. **The Editor letter is for the Editor, not the reviewers**: In your cover letter to the Editor, highlight what's new and improved. Make it easy for the Editor to see the value.

### When a Reviewer Requests an Infeasible Analysis

**Template for respectfully declining**:

> "We appreciate the reviewer's suggestion to [do X]. Unfortunately, [specific reason why X is infeasible—data limitation, sample size, methodological constraint]. Instead, we have [alternative analysis that addresses the same concern but is feasible], which we report in [section/table]. This analysis shows [result], consistent with the reviewer's intuition that [what makes the suggestion sensible]."

### Responding to the "So What?" Question

This is the most dreaded but most important reviewer comment. A reviewer who asks "so what?" is asking you to articulate the **managerial significance** of your finding:

**Strong Response**:
> "To demonstrate the managerial significance of our finding, we have added a back-of-the-envelope calculation (Section X, p. Y): If the average Fortune 500 retailer reallocates 10% of its promotional budget according to our recommendation, it would increase annual profit by an estimated $X million based on our estimated elasticity of Y. This calculation is meant to be illustrative, not definitive, but it demonstrates the order of magnitude of the managerial stakes."

## 6. Self-Review Checklist (Before Submission)

### Pre-Submission Audit: Act as Your Own Reviewer

**Substantive Contribution**:
- [ ] Can I state the single main contribution in one sentence without jargon?
- [ ] Does the paper address a real marketing problem a manager would recognize?
- [ ] Is the contribution clearly differentiated from the 8-12 closest papers?
- [ ] Does the differentiation table cover all dimensions of novelty?

**Identification Credibility** (for empirical papers):
- [ ] Is the source of exogenous variation clearly described with institutional detail?
- [ ] Are first-stage diagnostics convincing (F > 10, visual jump, event study)?
- [ ] Are all identifying assumptions explicitly stated?
- [ ] Are there placebo/falsification tests at different times, places, and outcomes?
- [ ] Is the balance table convincing (std. diff < 0.1 on all covariates)?

**Managerial Relevance**:
- [ ] Can I write one sentence that starts with "A marketing manager should..."?
- [ ] Are implications specific (not "improve targeting" but "target customers in the top 30% of..."?
- [ ] Is there a quantitative back-of-the-envelope calculation?
- [ ] Are implications connected to specific results?

**Methodological Rigor**:
- [ ] Are standard errors clustered at the correct level?
- [ ] Are multiple testing corrections applied where appropriate?
- [ ] Are there 8+ robustness checks covering alternative DVs, samples, specifications?
- [ ] Is the sensitivity to unobservables addressed (Oster, Rosenbaum bounds)?

**Positioning**:
- [ ] Have I cited 3-5 papers from the target journal published in the last 3 years?
- [ ] Does the literature review organize papers by stream, not paper-by-paper?
- [ ] Is the gap table clear and referenced in the text?

**Presentation**:
- [ ] Abstract within word limit (200 words for most journals)?
- [ ] Motivating example in first paragraph (real company, real numbers)?
- [ ] All variables defined at first use?
- [ ] All tables/figures cited in text?
- [ ] All references verified (no hallucinated citations)?

## 8. Understanding Review Outcomes

| Decision | What It Means | Next Steps |
|----------|--------------|------------|
| **Desk Reject** | Editor decided not to send for review | Revise and submit to different journal |
| **Reject** | After review, determined not suitable | Address major issues before resubmission (likely different journal) |
| **Reject & Resubmit** | Major problems but salvageable | Address ALL reviewer comments thoroughly; this is a "second chance" |
| **Major Revision** | Significant changes required | Most common outcome at good journals. Do everything asked. |
| **Minor Revision** | Small changes needed | Congratulations. This will almost certainly be accepted. |
| **Conditional Accept** | Only formatting/proofreading needed | Very rare on first round. Congratulations. |
| **Accept** | Ready for publication | Extremely rare on first round. |

**A "Major Revision" is a win**: At Marketing Science and JMR, a major revision is the most common positive outcome. It means the editor and reviewers see potential. Invest heavily in the revision—this is your best chance.
