## 1. Purpose and governing test

An abstract is written late, read first, and often decides whether the paper is opened. It must stand alone. A reader who sees nothing else should be able to explain the paper accurately to a colleague without receiving a false impression about the evidence.

The governing test is not whether the abstract mentions every familiar component. It is whether each sentence materially improves the reader's understanding of the paper's task, credibility or interpretability, findings, or closure.

## 2. The four reader questions

The abstract must let the reader recover:

1. What is the paper asking or resolving?
2. How does it answer the question?
3. What does it find?
4. What follows from the finding?

These are functions, not a four-sentence template. A sentence may perform two functions; a complex design may require more than one method sentence; a result may appear before the method; and the final function may be a theoretical boundary or mechanism rather than policy.

## 3. Opening

### Principle

The opening must perform substantive work immediately. It may:

- pose the research question;
- establish a real tension or policy tradeoff;
- state a headline finding;
- define a historical fact or economic setting essential to the question;
- introduce a specific model, measure, or empirical object.

### Rationale

Generic importance claims consume scarce space without distinguishing the paper. The first sentence should narrow the reader's uncertainty about this paper, not merely announce a broad topic.

### Avoid

- “Recently, X has attracted increasing attention.”
- “X is an important economic issue.”
- “This paper studies X” when `X` is only a broad label.
- literature-review openings and ceremonial gap claims.

### Important exception

Do not impose a lexical ban on `This paper` or `We examine`. An information-dense sentence such as “We develop a model that…” can be effective. The target is empty self-reference, not a pronoun. In the ten-paper calibration sample, several abstracts begin with `We` or `This paper` and immediately specify an economic object, design, or model.

## 4. Method and credibility

### Principle

Use method information to answer why the result is credible or what economic object the paper recovers. Do not display technical sophistication for its own sake.

### Causal empirical work

Identify the data or setting and the source of variation: random assignment, discontinuity, lottery, natural variation, instrument, or another design. Naming a method is insufficient if the reader still cannot tell what variation carries the claim.

### Associational or descriptive work

Describe the sample and measurement or comparison honestly. Do not invent identification language. Clarify what is observed and how the analysis organizes the relationship.

### Measurement work

State the object being measured and the selection, sorting, or comparability problem the method addresses.

### Structural or quantitative work

Name the core economic margins, data discipline, and counterfactual object. The method earns space by showing what the model makes possible.

### Theory

State the economic environment, key agents or mechanism, relevant cases, and conditions. “We build a model” without the economic content is empty.

### Compression rule

One sentence is often enough for a simple empirical design, but it is not a hard limit. Use two or more only when removing the detail would make the result uninterpretable or untrustworthy.

## 5. Findings

### Principle

Findings are the abstract's center of gravity. Report what the paper discovers, not merely what it does.

### Select findings by

- centrality to the main question;
- evidentiary strength;
- economic magnitude;
- surprise or contrast;
- value for defining a boundary;
- theoretical, welfare, or policy relevance;
- compressibility without distortion.

### Quantification

When the paper has an interpretable quantitative main result, report direction, magnitude, unit, and comparison or baseline. “Statistically significant” without economic content is weak. A reader should ideally retain one decision-relevant quantity.

Numbers are not compulsory. Theory papers, qualitative contrasts, or results that cannot be safely compressed should not be forced into false precision. Multiple numbers are acceptable only when they form one argument, not a table-like list.

### Contrasts, nulls, and boundaries

A null, reversal, heterogeneity result, or countervailing effect belongs when it changes the main claim. Stable high-level patterns in the Top Five sample include organizing findings around contrasts such as one treatment versus another, one group versus another, or a basic versus advanced model case.

### Mechanisms

Include a mechanism only if it is central and its evidence level can be expressed without exaggeration. Distinguish direct tests, exclusion evidence, patterns consistent with a channel, and model-implied mechanisms. Omit a weak mechanism when the necessary qualification would consume disproportionate space.

## 6. Closure and implications

### Principle

The ending should close the paper's core argument. It must answer a broader “so what?” without outrunning the evidence.

Possible closure functions include:

- a policy or welfare implication;
- a theoretical implication;
- a distributional consequence;
- a mechanism or parameter boundary;
- an external-validity qualification;
- a synthesis of opposing results;
- a robust comparison that changes interpretation.

Do not force a policy sentence. In the ten-paper calibration sample, final sentences perform many different closure functions. The stable rule is logical completion, not a fixed content category.

Avoid self-referential non-claims such as “We discuss policy implications.” State the actual supported implication or omit it.

## 7. Self-containment and exclusions

Normally exclude:

- literature citations;
- footnotes;
- `Section`, `Table`, `Figure`, or equation references;
- undefined abbreviations;
- routine robustness inventories;
- secondary findings that obscure the main line;
- empty actions such as “data are analyzed” or “theorems are proved” without the result;
- broad claims of importance or novelty unsupported by scope.

The abstract should not rely on the title or manuscript to decode it.

## 8. Exceptions by paper type

### Multi-result causal paper

Use a clear hierarchy: main treatment effect, the contrast or spillover that changes interpretation, one mechanism or boundary, and closure. Do not replay outcome tables.

### Structural policy paper

Lead with the tradeoff or decision problem, identify the model's key margins, report the counterfactual and its economically meaningful threshold or welfare implication, and expose important model dependence.

### Theory paper

Explain the framework and the comparison that generates the result. State conditions or distributional consequences when they define the contribution. Do not add data or identification language the paper lacks.

### Historical paper

A concise historical anchor may be essential. Then state the new evidence or design, central outcome, and mechanism or contemporary significance without turning the abstract into narrative exposition.

### Descriptive or measurement paper

Make the object and measurement problem concrete. Use descriptive or model-based language rather than implying a causal intervention.

## 9. Anti-patterns and repairs

| Failure | Why it fails | Repair |
|---|---|---|
| Generic importance opening | Does not distinguish the paper | Replace with the question, tension, object, or headline result |
| Four-sentence fill-in template | Produces mechanical order and omitted nuance | Plan functions, then merge or reorder according to the paper |
| Method-name list | Signals technique without credibility | State the variation, measurement problem, or economic object recovered |
| “Significant effect” | Omits economic content | Add a verified magnitude and baseline, or state a meaningful contrast |
| Result inventory | Treats all findings as equally important | Choose one main line and only boundary-changing secondary results |
| Unsupported mechanism | Turns interpretation into fact | Calibrate the evidence level or omit |
| Policy slogan | Exceeds external validity or welfare evidence | State a conditional implication or use another closure |
| Novelty adjective | Asserts rather than demonstrates contribution | Name the concrete data, design, model, or finding |
| Citations and internal references | Break self-containment and waste space | Remove and state the paper's own content directly |
| Vague compression | Becomes short by deleting meaning | Delete secondary content before credibility, main findings, or essential scope |

## 10. Rewrite and audit modes

When rewriting, preserve supported substance but do not preserve false promises. Compare the old abstract claim by claim with the body. Reordering and compression may be substantial; factual content must remain traceable.

When auditing, diagnose separately:

- unsupported or conflicting claims;
- missing core result or credibility source;
- overemphasized secondary material;
- causal and mechanism calibration;
- order and closure;
- length and density;
- natural academic English.

Do not rewrite an audit request unless the user asks for a revised version.
