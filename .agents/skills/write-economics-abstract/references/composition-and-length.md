# Abstract Composition and Elastic Length

## 1. Decide the information hierarchy

Rank eligible evidence before writing:

1. the research task or tension;
2. the credibility source, measurement solution, model, or theoretical mechanism needed to interpret the answer;
3. the main result;
4. a quantity or contrast that gives the result economic meaning;
5. a boundary-changing mechanism, null, heterogeneity, or countervailing result;
6. the strongest supported closure.

If the paper has several contributions, choose one spine. Secondary elements enter only when they change that spine.

Before sentence planning, assign eligible items to tiers. **Tier 1** contains the task or model tension, minimum model/data identity, primary result or counterfactual, and one boundary without which the answer would mislead. **Tier 2** contains secondary implications, decompositions, additional outcomes, and implementation quantities. **Tier 3** contains accounting incompatibilities, routine robustness detail, and facts whose main purpose is to explain why reported quantities cannot be combined. Complete Tier 1 first; include at most one Tier 2 item only if it changes the central interpretation; exclude Tier 3.

## 2. Default flow and permitted variations

Default:

`task or tension → how the paper answers → main result → boundary or mechanism → closure`

This is a logic dependency, not a sentence template.

Permitted variations:

- **Result first:** a headline result may open when it immediately defines the paper's intervention in a debate; explain credibility next.
- **Question and design merged:** common when the setting itself makes the question and design clear.
- **Design split:** use two or more sentences only when distinct identification sources or model margins are essential.
- **Closure merged with result:** useful for a short abstract where the implication follows directly.
- **Mechanism after policy or counterfactual:** acceptable when the mechanism explains the preceding implication.
- **No standalone policy sentence:** close with a theoretical condition, distributional result, external-validity boundary, or synthesis.

Do not reorder so aggressively that the reader encounters unexplained results, pronouns, samples, or models.

## 3. Paper-type plans

### Causal empirical

Question/tension → data and identifying variation → primary effect with magnitude → decisive spillover, heterogeneity, null, or mechanism → supported implication.

### Associational or descriptive

Object/question → data and measurement/comparison → central pattern and scale → boundary or interpretation → descriptive implication. Avoid causal drift.

### Measurement

Measurement target → sorting/selection/comparability problem → method and data → heterogeneity or validation → decision or policy use under stated conditions.

### Structural or quantitative

Economic tradeoff → only the model margins needed to understand it → data discipline in one compact unit → principal counterfactual → one decision-changing sensitivity or boundary → conditional interpretation. A model-implied association, decomposition, implementation cost, and welfare sensitivity must compete for space; do not include all four by default.

Preserve in this order: the economic problem, central margins or discipline, principal counterfactual, most decision-relevant tradeoff, and one qualification that prevents misreading. Cut estimation procedure, calibration-accounting detail, implementation cost, redundant decomposition, auxiliary counterfactuals, repeated model caveats, and a second scope caveat before cutting the central counterfactual or its boundary. Do not include two quantities merely to explain that they are incomparable; retain the more decision-relevant one unless non-comparability is itself the contribution.

### Theory

Framework and economic environment → key mechanism or cases → main theoretical comparison → distributional or parameter conditions → theoretical or organizational implication.

### Historical

Essential historical anchor → new data/design → central outcome → alternative-channel evidence or mechanism → historically grounded broader meaning.

## 4. Transitions

Prefer logical connections over stock signposts:

- method to result: state the estimate as what the design or model reveals;
- result to contrast: use the comparison to sharpen, not merely add;
- contrast to mechanism: explain why the difference matters;
- result to closure: state the implication that follows under the paper's scope.

Avoid mechanical labels such as `First`, `Second`, `Finally` unless the abstract genuinely contains a short ordered set.

## 5. Elastic length

Use a three-level decision:

### Default

The original guide treats roughly 100–150 words as the normal discipline. Use this range for a focused question, one principal design or framework, and one core result.

### Supported expansion

Roughly 150–200 words can be justified when the paper combines multiple essential identification sources, a structural model and counterfactual, theory and evidence, a central mechanism, or a boundary that prevents misreading.

The ten-paper calibration sample spans about 94–206 words, with a median near 121. This supports flexibility but does not replace the guide's compression norm.

### Compression alert

Above 200 words is a mandatory pre-delivery compression trigger, not merely a warning. Unless the user or outlet explicitly authorizes a longer form, rebuild the sentence plan from Tier 1, remove all Tier 3 material and all but one Tier 2 item, recount, and deliver 200 words or fewer. The 206-word calibration example demonstrates outlet variation; it does not authorize silent overage.

Very short abstracts around 90–125 words can work for one-design, one-result papers or strict journal limits. Do not achieve brevity by deleting the credibility source, central magnitude, or necessary scope.

Target-journal limits always prevail.

## 6. Information-density audit

For each sentence, ask:

- Does it change understanding of the task, credibility, result, boundary, or closure?
- Is it the shortest accurate version of that function?
- Does it duplicate another sentence?
- Does a number have an interpretable unit and baseline?
- Could a contrast replace a list?
- Is qualification necessary for truth, or merely habitual hedging?

Cut in this order: ceremonial background, generic contribution language, routine robustness, secondary outcomes, technical labels, repeated implications. Do not first cut the central result or the information that makes it credible.
