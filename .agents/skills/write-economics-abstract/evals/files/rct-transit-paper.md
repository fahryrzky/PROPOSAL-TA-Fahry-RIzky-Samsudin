# Working paper materials: Transit Subsidies and Secondary-School Persistence

> **Note:** This is a synthetic teaching and testing packet. All names, data, results, and citations are fictional.

## Research question

The paper asks whether short-run transportation liquidity constrains attendance and graduation among low-income secondary-school students.

## Setting, sample, and design

The study covers 2,400 students in grades 11 and 12 across 18 public high schools in one metropolitan district during the 2022–23 and 2023–24 school years. Eligible students were individually randomized within school and grade to receive either a prepaid public-transit card worth $30 per month or the status quo. Assignment, not card use, is the treatment. Administrative records cover attendance, lateness, disciplinary events, course grades, test scores, and on-time graduation. Card transaction data are available for treated students. Take-up was 72 percent.

## Main results

Intent-to-treat estimates show that assignment to the subsidy increased the attendance rate by 2.4 percentage points from a control mean of 86 percent and reduced the share of school days with late arrival by 1.8 percentage points from a 9 percent control mean. On-time graduation increased by 3.1 percentage points from a 74 percent control mean. Effects are concentrated among students living more than 45 minutes from school. The study finds no detectable effect on standardized test scores, course grades, or disciplinary events over the observed period.

## Mechanism evidence

Transit-card records show that treated students used the cards mainly on school days and during commuting hours. Reported door-to-door commute times did not change. The pattern is consistent with a transportation-liquidity channel that reduces missed or delayed trips, but the study does not separately randomize liquidity and therefore does not claim to identify the mechanism.

## Costs, limitations, and implications

The program cost the district about $180 per assigned student per school year. The paper does not estimate lifetime earnings, social welfare, or a benefit-cost ratio. It studies one district and two cohorts, so effects need not generalize to rural districts or places with limited transit coverage. The results show that modest transportation support can improve persistence in this setting; they do not establish that a universal transit subsidy is optimal.
