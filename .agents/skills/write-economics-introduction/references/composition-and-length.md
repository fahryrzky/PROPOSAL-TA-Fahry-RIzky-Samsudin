# Introduction Composition and Elastic Length

Use this reference to turn eligible manuscript evidence into a complete economics-paper introduction. Treat the sequence below as a chain of reader dependencies, not as a paragraph template. A function may occupy part of a paragraph or several paragraphs; adjacent functions may merge; a later function may be previewed early and developed after the reader has enough context.

## 1. Build a question-answer spine before drafting

Write one internal sentence for each item:

1. **Opening tension:** What specific fact, disagreement, tradeoff, measurement obstacle, or theoretical limitation makes the paper necessary?
2. **Research question:** What does the paper actually ask, for whom or what, in which setting, and over what scope?
3. **Answer:** What is the central finding, proposition, measurement result, or counterfactual conclusion?
4. **Credibility:** What data, variation, model discipline, archival construction, or measurement solution permits that answer?
5. **Meaning:** Why does the answer change an economic interpretation, a literature relationship, or a decision?

If these sentences do not form one coherent chain, do not solve the problem by adding background. Resolve the conflict in the manuscript evidence or ask the user for the missing information. The opening must create the question the paper answers; the results must answer that same question; the contribution must explain why that answer matters.

## 2. Use an early capsule followed by an extended proof

Most introductions in the calibration set operate in two passes.

The **early capsule** gives a busy reader the paper's identity: a concrete tension or obstacle, the bounded question, what the paper does, and the headline answer. It should arrive early enough that a reader can explain the paper without reading the entire introduction. This is the practical meaning of the inverted pyramid. It does not require the first sentence to report a result, nor does it require a mechanical two-paragraph limit. A short piece of background is useful when it creates the tension; it is throat-clearing when it only announces that a broad topic is important.

The **extended proof** earns the headline answer. It supplies the setting, data, identifying variation, model, estimand, assumptions, main results, interpretation, and literature relationship in the order the reader needs them. The more contestable the claim or complex the design, the more credibility work belongs here. Do not assume that one method sentence is sufficient for a multi-instrument, structural, historical-linkage, or selection-correction paper.

## 3. Default functional progression

A reliable default is:

`specific tension or obstacle -> bounded question -> early paper-and-answer capsule -> credibility architecture -> ordered findings -> interpretation and necessary boundaries -> contribution and literature relationship -> optional roadmap`

This ordering reflects dependencies:

- A hook earns attention only when it leads directly to the research question.
- The question gives the method and results an object.
- The credibility architecture gives the reader a reason to interpret the findings as the paper does.
- Findings precede their broader meaning, so the implication is derived rather than asserted.
- Detailed literature positioning is easier to understand after the reader knows the paper. Targeted early citations may still establish a fact, debate, measurement failure, or theoretical limitation.
- The roadmap is navigation, not part of the intellectual claim.

Permitted variations include a result-first opening when the result itself defines the puzzle; a setting-first opening when the institution creates the design; a measurement-opportunity opening when the missing outcome is the research problem; and an embedded section-by-section tour when a quantitative paper's theory, identification, estimates, and policy exercise form the argument. Preserve dependency clarity under every variation.

## 4. Organize findings as an economic argument

Do not recap tables or follow the manuscript's regression order. Select a result path such as:

`primary answer -> economically important contrast or decomposition -> evidence about why or when -> boundary or null that changes interpretation -> welfare, policy, theoretical, or historical meaning`

For empirical and quantitative work, report exact magnitudes when they are central and interpretable. Preserve the outcome, unit, denominator, sample, time horizon, comparison, uncertainty, and causal status. Add a benchmark only when the manuscript supports it. For theory, replace the demand for a number with a clear conditional proposition, comparison, and intuition.

### Admit second-layer results by value, not by category

Mechanisms, heterogeneity, null results, robustness, alternative identification, validation, and external-validity evidence are not automatically excluded from an introduction. Admit one when it does at least one of the following:

- changes the interpretation of the primary answer;
- addresses the principal threat to credibility;
- distinguishes the paper's mechanism from a live alternative;
- defines an important population, parameter, institutional, or model boundary;
- connects an empirical estimate to a structural or policy conclusion;
- prevents a headline result from being materially misleading.

Exclude it when it is routine, exploratory, weakly estimated, duplicative, or present only because it appears in the manuscript. Never let secondary evidence displace the primary answer. Match language to evidence: a suggestive subgroup pattern is not an established mechanism; a decomposition is not causal identification; a model implication is not an observed fact; a counterfactual is conditional on the model and scenario.

Treat source-labeled exploratory findings and partial implementation-cost figures as omitted by default. An exploratory finding enters only when leaving it out would materially misstate the main answer or an indispensable boundary. A cost number enters only when implementation economics is central to the paper's question or contribution, or when the user or journal specifically requires it. Spending a paragraph on qualifications does not convert a low-priority item into an introduction function.

Apply the same selection discipline to limitations. State each indispensable limit once, adjacent to the claim it qualifies. Repeating noncausal status, model dependence, mechanism uncertainty, or sample selection across several paragraphs turns the introduction into an audit and crowds out the research story.

## 5. Paragraph progression and transitions

Give each paragraph a job that advances the question-answer chain. Useful paragraph jobs include opening a tension, narrowing the question, presenting the early capsule, explaining a source of credibility, reporting one connected result cluster, evaluating a mechanism or boundary, locating one contribution, or providing navigation.

Vary paragraph length with argumentative load. A short paragraph can state a sharp bottom line; a longer paragraph may be needed to explain an identification threat or structural margin. Do not split prose merely to reach a target count, and do not let a long paragraph contain several unrelated jobs.

Transitions should name real logic:

- tension to question: state what remains unresolved;
- question to method: explain why the design or model can answer it;
- method to result: state what the credible variation, estimation, or equilibrium reveals;
- result to mechanism: identify the explanation being tested and its evidentiary status;
- result to contribution: compare the new answer with the verified prior benchmark;
- contribution to roadmap: move from intellectual payoff to navigation only if navigation is useful.

Avoid ceremonial transitions and repeated announcements that the paper is important. Enumeration such as `First`, `Second`, and `Third` is useful only when the results form a genuine ordered set.

## 6. Make the roadmap conditional

Include a roadmap when the paper has several empirical strategies, a theory-to-estimation-to-counterfactual sequence, nonstandard section responsibilities, or a target outlet that expects one. Keep it short and identify each section's substantive task rather than saying only that it contains data, methods, or results.

Only use section numbers and responsibilities supplied by the manuscript or user. Complexity may make a roadmap desirable, but it cannot reveal a structure that has not been provided.

Omit the roadmap when the structure is conventional and already obvious, when space is scarce, or when it merely repeats the preceding paper tour. An introduction may also embed section references in the argument when the sequence of sections is itself the logic of the paper. Do not add a roadmap solely because other papers have one.

## 7. Use elastic length, not a word quota

The repaired ten-paper calibration contains introductions of approximately 1,760 to 2,978 words, with a median near 2,265 words. Eight lie between about 1,760 and 2,489 words; two complex papers lie near 2,824 to 2,978 words. Main-text paragraph counts range from roughly 13 to 33 because journal layout and paragraph style vary greatly. These observations calibrate information load; they do not establish a universal Top Five requirement.

Use three practical bands:

- **Focused introduction, about 1,700-1,900 words:** one bounded question, one main design or framework, a compact result path, and limited positioning burden.
- **Developed introduction, about 2,000-2,500 words:** the normal choice when the reader must understand the setting, credibility architecture, several connected findings, one important mechanism or boundary, and multiple literature relationships.
- **Extended introduction, about 2,700-3,000 words:** justified by multiple identification strategies, an empirical-to-structural mapping, several indispensable model margins, a new measurement object, unusually demanding historical construction, or several counterfactual stages.

Target-journal practice and explicit user limits prevail. Shorten when the paper has one question, one design, and one result spine; expand only when additional material helps the reader understand, trust, or correctly delimit the answer. Do not make an introduction longer because the manuscript is long, nor shorter by deleting the information that makes its central claim credible.

### Calibration source boundary

The ninth calibration paper must be read from the complete introduction in the original paper PDF, ending at the `Theory` section. The derived bilingual booklet and extracted text stop early at a page break and omit most of its identification, results, policy, extension, and literature discussion. Use the complete original for length, sequence, and type calibration; treat the shortened derivative as incomplete evidence. Do not generalize its apparent brevity.

## 8. Compression and expansion audit

Before delivery, test every paragraph:

- Does it establish the question, provide the answer, earn credibility, interpret a first-order result, calibrate a necessary boundary, locate a verified contribution, or provide useful navigation?
- Does any background sentence merely delay the paper capsule?
- Is each result attached to the design, model, or measurement object that supports it?
- Does a second-layer result alter the central interpretation enough to justify its space?
- Does the literature discussion compare claims rather than list authors?
- Is the opening tension resolved by the paper's bottom line?

Compress first: generic importance claims, institutional detail that does not affect the question or design, routine robustness, exploratory heterogeneity, repeated results, unverified novelty language, citation inventories, and a redundant roadmap. Expand only to repair a missing dependency in the question-answer-credibility-meaning chain.
