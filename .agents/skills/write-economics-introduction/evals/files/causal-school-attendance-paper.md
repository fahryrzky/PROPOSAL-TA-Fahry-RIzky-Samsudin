# Synthetic manuscript packet: timely attendance alerts

This is an original development case. All names and citations below are fictional and exist only inside this packet.

## Current body facts

The study asks whether sending timely, personalized attendance alerts to caregivers changes secondary-school attendance. The setting is 72 public-school districts in one country during the 2022–2023 academic year. Districts, not caregivers or students, were randomly assigned in equal proportions to the alert program or business as usual. In treated districts, the education ministry's messaging system sent caregivers a text within 24 hours after an unexcused absence. The experimental sample contains 18,420 students in grades 7–10 whose caregivers had a verified mobile number before randomization.

The preregistered primary outcome is the share of enrolled school days attended over the academic year. Assignment to the alert program increased attendance by 1.6 percentage points from a control mean of 88.4 percent. The 95 percent confidence interval is 0.8 to 2.4 percentage points. The program also reduced the probability of chronic absence, defined before treatment as missing at least 10 percent of enrolled days, by 3.2 percentage points from a 19.0 percent control mean.

The paper does not estimate effects on learning. End-of-year standardized tests are available for only 61 percent of the experimental sample because several districts did not administer the common test. In that selected test sample, the estimated effect on the standardized score is 0.02 standard deviations, with a 95 percent confidence interval from -0.03 to 0.07. The manuscript consistently calls this “no detectable test-score effect in the observed test sample,” not “no effect on learning.”

Portal logs show that caregivers in treated districts were more likely to open the attendance dashboard, and a post-program survey reports greater knowledge of recent absences. These patterns are consistent with reduced information delays. The experiment does not separately randomize information timing, dashboard access, or caregiver attention, so the paper does not identify the information mechanism.

An exploratory subgroup analysis finds a larger attendance estimate for students with above-median prior absence. The analysis was not preregistered and the interaction confidence interval is wide. The paper treats it as exploratory, not as a central result.

The implementation cost recorded by the ministry is USD 2.10 per student assigned to treatment. The manuscript does not include school staff time, caregiver time, long-run benefits, or a benefit-cost calculation. It therefore does not claim the program is cost-effective.

## Design details

- Assignment unit: school district.
- Treatment recipient: caregivers with verified mobile numbers in treated districts.
- Delivery actor: the education ministry messaging system.
- Observation unit: student-day, aggregated to the student-year.
- Analysis and inference: student-level regressions with district-level clustered standard errors.
- Identified treatment: assignment of districts to the timely attendance-alert program.
- Candidate mechanism: reduced delay in caregivers' information about absences.

## Verified literature packet

The following summaries were prepared from the cited papers' full texts for this synthetic case.

**Mendez and Luo (2019), “Caregiver Information and Student Presence.”** A field experiment in 18 urban middle schools sent monthly attendance summaries. It found a 0.7 percentage-point increase in attendance. It did not test messages sent immediately after an absence.

**Okafor, Stein, and Vega (2021), “Administrative Frictions in School Communication.”** A descriptive study documents that attendance notices commonly arrive two to four weeks after an absence. It reports associations between notification delay and caregiver knowledge but does not estimate an intervention effect.

**Baner and Cho (2022), “Text Messages, Attendance, and Achievement.”** A randomized experiment sent weekly generic reminders to parents in 40 elementary schools. It found a 0.9 percentage-point attendance effect and no detectable math-score effect. The study concerns elementary schools and generic weekly reminders.

Safe positioning supported by this packet: prior experiments show that caregiver messages can improve attendance; the current paper studies a different margin—personalized alerts within 24 hours—in a secondary-school setting and with district-level randomization. The packet does not establish that this is the first such study worldwide.
