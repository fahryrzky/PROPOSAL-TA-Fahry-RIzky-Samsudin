# Output contract

Adapt detail to the user's input, but preserve the decision logic below.

## 1. Executive diagnosis

- Raw idea in one sentence.
- Current contribution level: application, dataset, quantification, metric, pattern, causal effect, mechanism, general principle, or decision consequence.
- Best-supported direction and why.
- Weakest link preventing a stronger story.

## 2. Literature reference matrix

Present 6–12 verified papers where feasible. Use the fields defined in `literature-workflow.md`. Then summarize:

- established territory;
- transferable story moves;
- differentiation opportunity;
- search/access limitations.

## 3. Evidence ledger

Separate observed/computed, designed, inferred, assumed, and wanted items. Add claim tags where useful.

Also provide a user-knowledge audit when the user supplies substantive scientific content:

| User-provided claim | Status | Independent evidence | Disciplinary nuance | Action |
|---|---|---|---|---|

Use statuses such as supported, partly supported, disputed, outdated, incorrect, normative, and unresolved. Do not silently correct or silently adopt a consequential claim.

## 4. Competing story versions

Give at least three versions in a comparison table:

| Version | Central question | Expectation challenged | Story spine | Decisive evidence | Fatal weakness | Effort/risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Use meaningful names, not just A/B/C. Include conservative, mechanism-first, consequence-first, and high-upside/high-risk variants when warranted.

For each version write the spine:

> Because X, we ask Y; we find Z, identify mechanism M, establish boundary B, and show consequence C.

Do not fill unknown Z, M, B, or C with invented results. Use conditional wording and label the needed test.

## 5. Story stress test

Score the dimensions in `SKILL.md`, explain each score, identify the weakest link, and show what result would change the ranking. Do not let a numerical total conceal a fatal flaw.

## 6. Recommended research programme

For the top candidates:

- essential analyses;
- strong enhancers;
- optional/supplementary work;
- falsification and competing-explanation tests;
- data/method dependencies;
- stop/go criteria;
- one killer analysis;
- one analysis to delete or demote.

Include a short claim–evidence table:

| Claim | Type | Needed evidence | Current status | Main threat |
|---|---|---|---|---|

## 7. Figure architecture

For every main figure state:

- reader question;
- one-sentence claim;
- analysis and counterfactual;
- uncertainty and robustness;
- role in the story transition;
- content to move to Supplementary Information.

Prefer 4–6 main figures, but let the science determine the number.

## 8. Results section architecture

For the recommended story, always propose **exactly four or five Results section titles**. Present them in manuscript order:

| Results section title | Question answered | Claim advanced | Main figure/analysis | Evidence status | Transition to next section |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Requirements:

- Make titles concise, specific, and claim-led rather than method-led.
- Ensure the sequence advances one coherent story rather than collecting independent findings.
- Map every title to evidence and, where possible, a main figure.
- Use the manuscript language; provide bilingual titles only when requested.
- If a result is not yet established, label the title **provisional** and use conditional or prospective wording rather than fabricating a finding.
- Do not use generic placeholders such as “Results 1”, “Spatial patterns”, “Model evaluation”, or “Policy analysis” as the final titles.

A typical five-part logic is phenomenon → expectation failure or tension → mechanism → boundary/heterogeneity → consequence or intervention. A four-part version may merge adjacent functions when that produces a tighter argument. Adapt the logic to the science rather than forcing every archetype into the same sequence.

## 9. Decision and next step

End with:

- recommended version now;
- why the other versions rank lower;
- next analysis with the highest information gain;
- minimum inputs needed from the user;
- conditions that would force a narrower claim or a different story.

## Tone

Be candid, specific, and generative. Strong ideas can still have fatal weaknesses. Weak ideas can still yield valuable papers without being inflated into broad claims. Use Chinese or English to match the user; preserve standard technical terms when translation would reduce precision.
