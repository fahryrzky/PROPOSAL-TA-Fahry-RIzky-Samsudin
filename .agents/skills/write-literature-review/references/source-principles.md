# Source Principles: Writing Critical Literature Synthesis

Distilled from the two sources in `Advice for Academic Finance/Write review/`, plus the user's draft workflow spec. Read this when you need to judge an edge case the SKILL.md workflow doesn't explicitly cover — the *reasoning* lives here.

**Adaptation note:** Both PDFs address standalone review articles. A literature review *section* of an empirical paper is an abbreviated, positioning-driven application: its job is to establish what is known, what is contested, and why the paper's contribution is marginal and necessary — not to survey the field comprehensively.

## Sources

1. "How to write an excellent Review article." *Nature Reviews Bioengineering* (Editorial), November 2024. DOI 10.1038/s44222-024-00256-4.
2. Dhillon, P. (2022). "How to write a good scientific review article." *The FEBS Journal* 289, 3592–3602. DOI 10.1111/febs.16565.

## 1. Timeliness and angle (Nat. Rev. Bioeng.)

- A synthesis is worth writing only when the underlying literature has enough recent primary research to justify a critical discussion. Their heuristic: ~30 relevant primary papers in the past 2–3 years (for a full review). For a paper's lit review section, the analogous question: are there active, ongoing debates in each stream you cite, or is the stream settled background?
- Find the compelling angle before writing: has the field shifted focus, do new results raise fresh questions, is a translational/policy gap opening? The angle — not chronology — should shape the narrative.
- Identify key findings, open questions, emerging insights, controversies, and recent conclusions *before* outlining. The outline is built from these, not from a topic list.

## 2. Scope discipline (Dhillon 2022, Fig. 1)

Six factors when defining scope — mapped to a paper's lit review section:

| Factor | Review article | Lit review section |
|---|---|---|
| Timeliness | Enough recent primary research | Each cited stream is an active debate |
| Novelty | No close recent review exists | The positioning angle differs from recent papers' own lit reviews |
| Breadth | Manageable but draws readership | 2–4 streams, deliberately weighted |
| Author expertise | Deep working knowledge | Credibility to adjudicate conflicts between papers |
| Journal relevance | Fits the journal's community | Frame streams the target journal's readers recognize |
| Motivation | Sustained interest through a long project | Usually not binding for a short section |

- The most common scope mistake: too ambitious, covering everything superficially. A review section isn't a thesis or textbook chapter (Dhillon). If it reads like one, cut streams.
- Work backwards from constraints: length limit, journal conventions (dedicated section vs. woven into the introduction).

## 3. Synthesis vs. laundry list (both sources — the central warning)

- "Do not merely list research outcomes in chronological order; instead, compare and contrast the most representative research findings, exploring what has succeeded, what has not, and what can be learned from any failures. Look for common themes, conflicting conclusions and bottlenecks." (Nat. Rev. Bioeng.)
- "Don't be tempted to write a review in the style of a laundry list, with different sets of findings briefly described with minimal links, discussion and interpretation. This can be repetitive, dull and ineffective at driving a central point home to readers... explain the context and broader implications of a body of research in a way that underlines how it has advanced the field and also brings to light the gaps in current knowledge, rather than merely regurgitating the facts." (Dhillon)
- Practical test: if a paragraph can be summarized as "X found this; Y found that; Z found the other," it is a laundry list. Rewrite around a claim the paragraph defends.
- You may need to be *selective* — summarizing every relevant paper meaningfully is usually impossible (Dhillon). In a section, selectivity is not a vice; comprehensive-but-shallow is.

## 4. Explaining conflicts (user's draft workflow)

When findings conflict, check whether the difference comes from: research setting, sample, sample size, measurement definitions, model assumptions, statistical power, publication vintage, or conflicting interests. If you cannot explain the conflict, keep the uncertainty explicit — do not manufacture a resolution.

## 5. Balance and citation practice (Dhillon 2022)

- Present all pertinent findings even if you build toward a particular viewpoint. Discuss findings you disagree with.
- Don't cite only your own work / your camp's work — reviewers notice and it diminishes authority.
- "Cite, cite, cite": most fact-based statements need a source. Cite the *original* research paper reporting a finding, not a later review that discussed it — this assigns credit correctly and doesn't mislead readers on the history of the discovery.
- No ambiguity about who performed the work when describing a study.
- Never copy source sentences, even well-worded ones, including from your own earlier papers (text recycling). Extract facts and relations, re-express in new structure and language.

## 6. Reader-friendliness (both sources)

- The audience may include readers unfamiliar with the subfield: explain mechanisms and concepts clearly, but keep it simple (Nat. Rev. Bioeng.).
- Avoid acronyms and abbreviations — meanings vary across fields (Nat. Rev. Bioeng.); define any that survive at first use (Dhillon).
- Never assume the reader sees the link between two bodies of research or why the section moves from one theme to the next: make transitions explicit (Dhillon).
- Sentences and paragraphs concise; a paragraph carries one judgment; topic sentence gives direction, closing sentence gives implication or turn (draft workflow).
- Be as specific as possible: models, systems, methodologies, magnitudes (Nat. Rev. Bioeng. adapted to finance: data, sample period, identification strategy, effect sizes).
- Make clear what is established finding vs. hypothesis vs. this paper's inference (Nat. Rev. Bioeng. + draft workflow's epistemic distinctions).

## 7. Display items (both sources, adapted)

- Tables that compare systems, metrics, or approaches are valuable (Nat. Rev. Bioeng.). In finance lit reviews, a comparison table of closely related papers (question, data, method, finding, difference from this paper) is a legitimate and common display item when 3+ papers are method-comparable.
- A figure/diagram must do structured explanation, cross-study comparison, or mechanism integration — if one sentence can replace it, don't make it (draft workflow).

## 8. Narrative arc (Dhillon 2022 + Nat. Rev. Bioeng.)

- Each subsection independently introduced and concluded (Dhillon). In a lit review section: each thread opens with its claim and closes with the positioning move — how this paper differs.
- The concluding move of the whole section: summarize what is established, name the gap, hand off to the paper's contribution. Speculation belongs in the paper's conclusion, not the lit review (inverted from review-article advice where the outlook section may speculate freely).
- Title discipline transfers to subsection headings: specific and revealing, not "Recent developments" or "Literature" (Nat. Rev. Bioeng. bans vague terms like "advances," "emerging," "recent").

## 9. Process discipline (Dhillon 2022)

- Plan before writing: outline with key references noted under each heading. "Having planned your review article meticulously... the actual writing part should be relatively easy."
- Build the bibliography as you work; keep updating throughout the process; set up alerts for new papers during long writing cycles.
- Related papers go under the same subheading with links between them made explicit — form a coherent narrative.
- Take breaks and return with fresh eyes; expect several iterations.
- Get feedback from colleagues, including non-specialists — if they can't follow the section, the target reader may not either.
