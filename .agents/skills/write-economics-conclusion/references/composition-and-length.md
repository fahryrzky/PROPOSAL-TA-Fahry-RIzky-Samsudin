# Conclusion Composition, Module Selection, and Elastic Length

## 1. Start with the takeaway

Write one internal sentence answering: what is the single claim the reader should carry away? Select evidence and modules only after this sentence is stable.

The conclusion must contain a distilled answer. Other modules are conditional.

## 2. Select modules by value

Use a module only if it changes the reader's final interpretation.

| Module | Include when | Omit when |
|---|---|---|
| Re-anchor and distilled findings | Always | Never omit the core answer |
| Mechanism/economic intuition | Supported and central to why/when | Merely speculative or already requires long qualification |
| Heterogeneity/null/boundary | Prespecified or otherwise central and changes scope or interpretation | Exploratory subgroup, routine inventory, imprecise interaction, or any pattern requiring speculative explanation |
| Limitation | Specific and tied to a claim | Generic or invented |
| Robustness response | Calibrates a named threat | A checklist or false proof of certainty |
| Policy/welfare/theory implication | Derived under supported scope and assumptions | Aspirational or unrelated |
| Future research | Concrete and grown from an identified boundary | Generic or agent-invented |
| Literature/institutional connection | Performs necessary interpretation | Recreates literature review |

Most conclusions need the core answer plus two to four selected modules, not every module.

Exploratory, unregistered, weakly estimated, or secondary heterogeneity is excluded by default. Include it only when it changes the central interpretation, its evidentiary qualification can appear in the same sentence, and it does not displace a first-order result. No secondary, robustness, or exploratory module may receive a standalone paragraph or more explanatory space than the primary findings it qualifies.

## 3. Default flow and local attachment

Default:

`re-anchor → selected findings → mechanism or economic intuition → boundary calibration → implication or future direction`

Use this as a dependency structure. Variations:

- Put a limitation directly after the claim it qualifies.
- Combine mechanism and heterogeneity to explain when the result holds.
- Move a central implication immediately after the main result, then state its limits.
- Merge a boundary with a concrete future direction.
- End with a boundary rather than a broad implication when restraint is the paper's most important message.
- End with a future agenda, policy judgment, theoretical lesson, external-validity condition, or historical meaning according to the paper.

Results should normally precede extensions so the reader knows what the later discussion is interpreting.

## 4. Paper-type plans

### Focused empirical paper

One short re-anchor and answer → one or two result sentences → mechanism or boundary if supported → restrained implication or concrete future question.

Do not spend a full paragraph on exploratory heterogeneity when primary results, a quality boundary, implementation facts, or external-validity limits already supply the first-order takeaway.

### Multi-result causal paper

Synthesize how the results fit together → explain the central channel or contrast → calibrate causal and external scope → state one implication. Do not list outcomes in regression order.

### Structural or policy paper

Restate the decision problem and model object → synthesize data discipline and counterfactual → explain welfare or policy tradeoff → expose uncertainty, missing margins, or model dependence → state conditional use.

### Theory paper

Revisit the motivating problem → organize a few broad lessons → state mechanisms, comparisons, distributional effects, and parameter conditions → identify model boundaries and next theoretical margins.

### Historical paper

Re-anchor the historical episode and evidence → synthesize causal or descriptive findings → explain channels and under/overstatement risks → connect to broader history or the present only where justified.

### Measurement paper

Revisit the measurement target → state what heterogeneity or sorting the measure reveals → explain practical use → identify implementation or external-validity work still needed.

## 5. Transitions

Build conceptual rather than ceremonial transitions:

- findings to mechanism: `This pattern is consistent with...` only when the evidence level warrants it;
- mechanism to boundary: state which group, condition, or assumption weakens the channel;
- boundary to future research: name the evidence needed to resolve it;
- findings to policy: identify the exact tradeoff the result informs;
- model to counterfactual: make assumptions visible when interpretation depends on them.

Avoid generic pivots such as `The study has several limitations` or `There are important policy implications` without immediately specifying them.

After `is consistent with`, state only the channel supported by the manuscript. Do not append an unreported explanation for why that channel should be stronger in a subgroup or setting.

## 6. Elastic length

Use information load, paper length, journal style, and whether Discussion is separate.

### Focused default

The original guide's discipline is “short and sweet”; for a roughly 20-page paper, about one single-spaced page is often sufficient. A focused paper may need only about 120–250 words.

### Standard developed conclusion

About 250–450 words often accommodates a re-anchor, synthesis, one explanation or boundary, and a meaningful ending. In the ten-paper calibration sample, the median is about 350–370 words and most non-extreme cases fall around 249–466.

### Supported expansion

About 300–550 words can be appropriate for multi-result causal, structural, quantitative, or theoretical papers. A combined Discussion/Conclusion may extend to roughly 600–900 words when it must resolve identification concerns, policy design, institutional detail, model conditions, or several genuine boundaries.

One calibration paper reaches roughly 1,028 words. Treat this as a complex exception, not permission to be long. Above the normal range, verify that expansion adds interpretation rather than another result or literature review.

Target-journal rules and separate Discussion conventions prevail.

## 7. Expansion and compression tests

Expand only to:

- explain a central mechanism or economic intuition;
- calibrate a consequential limitation;
- interpret a counterfactual or welfare tradeoff;
- distinguish conditions under which the conclusion changes;
- derive a concrete future agenda.

Compress first:

- repeated abstract or introduction language;
- table-by-table recap;
- routine robustness;
- generic literature positioning;
- decorative policy language;
- future-work lists not tied to boundaries.

Every paragraph should either preserve the central takeaway, explain it, calibrate it, or derive a supported next implication.
