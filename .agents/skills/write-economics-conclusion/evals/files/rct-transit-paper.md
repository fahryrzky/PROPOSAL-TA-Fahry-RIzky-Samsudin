# Working paper materials: Transit Subsidies and Secondary-School Persistence

> **Note:** This is a synthetic teaching and testing packet. All names, data, results, and citations are fictional.

## Research question and design

The paper asks whether transportation liquidity constrains school persistence. It follows 2,400 grade 11–12 students in 18 public high schools in one metropolitan district over two school years. Students were randomized within school and grade to a $30 monthly transit card or the status quo. Take-up was 72 percent.

## Results

Intent-to-treat assignment increased attendance by 2.4 percentage points from an 86 percent control mean, reduced late-arrival days by 1.8 percentage points from a 9 percent mean, and increased on-time graduation by 3.1 percentage points from a 74 percent mean. Effects are concentrated among students living more than 45 minutes from school. Test scores, grades, and disciplinary events show no detectable effect.

## Mechanism and limits

Card use occurs mainly on school days and commuting hours, while reported commute time does not change. This is consistent with reduced transportation liquidity constraints but does not separately identify the channel. The program costs about $180 per assigned student-year. The study does not estimate lifetime earnings, welfare, or benefit-cost ratios. It covers one urban district with developed public transit and two cohorts.

## Supported discussion and future direction

The paper argues that modest transportation support can improve persistence in this setting, but does not claim a universal subsidy is optimal. A natural next step stated in the discussion is to test districts with weaker transit networks and to distinguish fare liquidity from route availability. The authors do not propose mandates.
