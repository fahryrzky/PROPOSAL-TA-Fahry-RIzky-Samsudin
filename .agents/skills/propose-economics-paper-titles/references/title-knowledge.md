## 1. Purpose and governing test

The title is the paper's most frequently seen sentence and its first contract with the reader. It should be accurate, distinctive, concise, professional, and memorable. Its goal is to attract the right reader by revealing what the paper is, not to maximize clicks or announce that the paper is important.

The governing test is contractual: the body must deliver every substantive promise made by the title.

## 2. What a title should contain

An effective title carries at least one substantive source of identity:

- the research topic or economic object;
- the central relationship or task;
- a core finding;
- a genuine tension or contrast;
- a distinctive method, measurement object, design, or setting.

It need not carry all of these. A clear topic title can be excellent. A finding or tension strengthens a title only when it is central and safe to advertise.

Do not summarize the entire paper in the title. The title should let the relevant reader recognize the paper and distinguish it from adjacent work.

## 3. Finalize late

Research questions, results, and contributions change. Treat the title as provisional until the body and central contribution stabilize. Before finalizing, answer:

1. What should the reader remember?
2. Which object, relationship, contrast, design, or setting differentiates the paper?
3. What is the strongest one-sentence thesis the body actually supports?
4. What must the title not promise?

Generate from that thesis rather than from early project keywords.

## 4. Information families

Use families as conditional options, not a checklist.

### Topic or concept

Name the central object or relationship directly. Best when the paper's subject is already distinctive and the design or result need not occupy title space.

### Contrast or tension

Expose opposing forces, cases, or levels when the contrast organizes the actual paper. A verbal contrast is useful only if the body develops both sides.

### Conceptual hook plus precise subtitle

A short, meaningful hook can carry historical, conceptual, or visual weight; the subtitle must ground it in the research object. Avoid puns or mystery unrelated to the analysis.

### Design or setting

Include a design or context when it is a genuine source of credibility, the substantive object, or a necessary scope boundary. A location is not automatically informative because the data were collected there.

### Model, measurement, or theory–evidence task

Advertise a distinctive measurement target, model object, counterfactual, or integration of theory and data when that is the paper's identity.

### Result-oriented title

Use only when the result is central, robust, stable across the manuscript, and not misleading without qualifications. The ten-paper calibration sample contains no titles with numeric estimates or explicit result directions; this is a caution, not a prohibition.

### Question title

Use only when both answers are plausible, the tension is substantive, and the paper answers the question clearly. The original guide permits this form, but none of the ten calibration titles uses it. Do not manufacture a question for variety.

## 5. `Evidence from` and scope

`Evidence from` is neither a quality signal nor a forbidden phrase. It earns space when what follows conveys distinctive identification, a substantive case, or an important boundary.

High-information possibilities include:

- random assignment;
- a distinctive natural experiment or institution;
- an industry or historical setting that defines the economic mechanism;
- a population essential to external validity.

Low-information uses include a generic country label that says only where data happened to be available.

Run the deletion test: if removing `Evidence from ...` loses no important design or scope information, delete or replace it.

The same test applies to countries, years, industries, and institutions. Include them only when they define identification, interpretation, or audience.

## 6. Consistency and causal language

Compare the title with the body, abstract, introduction opening, and conclusion takeaway. Check:

- Does the paper truly answer the title's question?
- Does it develop the advertised tension?
- Does the design support `impact`, `effect`, or other causal wording?
- Is the mechanism established strongly enough to name?
- Is the population or setting narrower than the title implies?
- Is the title centered on the delivered contribution rather than a literature-review aspiration?

When evidence is associational, prefer objects and relationships over causal verbs. When the scope is local, do not write a universal title merely because it sounds cleaner.

## 7. Compression and memorability

The guide's roughly 10–12-word target is a compression heuristic, not a hard cap. Every extra word should buy truth, distinction, or necessary scope. Every deletion should be tested for lost meaning.

Accuracy outranks cleverness. Distinctive concept combinations are usually more durable than promotional adjectives. A longer title can be justified when it must preserve a core comparison, mechanism, outcome domain, or setting.

Colon structure can separate a memorable concept from a precise task, contrast, design, or scope. It works only when the two halves perform different functions. The ten-paper sample contains many colon titles, but that small-sample frequency does not create a colon preference.

Avoid `new`, `novel`, `important`, `first`, and similar self-appraisal unless a narrow claim is verified. Let concrete content signal contribution.

## 8. Anti-patterns and repairs

| Failure | Why it fails | Repair |
|---|---|---|
| Broad label | Says little beyond a field keyword | Add the central relationship, task, contrast, or object |
| Keyword pile | Tries to summarize everything | Keep the few elements that define the paper |
| Generic `Evidence from [country]` | Uses scarce title space without identification or scope value | Replace with substantive design/context or remove |
| Causal wording for observational evidence | Overpromises the design | Use neutral relationship or object language |
| Secondary mechanism in title | Misidentifies the paper | Return to the central thesis and evidence hierarchy |
| Clever but ungrounded hook | Attracts the wrong reader | Use a concept naturally tied to the paper or remove it |
| Redundant colon halves | Repeats rather than narrows | Give each half a distinct function |
| Forced question | Creates tension the paper does not have | Use topic, task, contrast, or design form |
| Promotional adjective | Substitutes self-evaluation for information | Show the contribution through concrete content |
| Overcompression | Deletes a necessary comparison or scope boundary | Restore the word that prevents a false promise |
| Excessive candidate similarity | Gives cosmetic choice | Change information architecture, not synonyms |

## 9. Rewrite, compare, and audit modes

When rewriting an existing title, identify supported elements worth retaining. Do not assume novelty requires total replacement. Generate alternatives only after diagnosing the current contract.

When comparing candidates, score each on truth, central fit, distinction, density, rhythm, and length—in that order. Explain the main tradeoff instead of declaring a winner by taste.

When auditing, diagnose:

- unsupported promises;
- causal and mechanism strength;
- scope accuracy;
- centrality;
- information density;
- distinctiveness;
- title-family fit;
- unnecessary words and lost necessary words.

Do not silently replace an audit request with a candidate list.
