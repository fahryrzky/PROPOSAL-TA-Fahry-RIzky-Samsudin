# Title Generation, Ranking, and Elastic Length

## 1. Build the title brief

Before generating candidates, write a private brief:

- one-sentence conservative thesis;
- indispensable research object(s);
- central relationship, task, or contrast;
- evidence type and causal ceiling;
- central result, if stable enough to advertise;
- model, measurement, design, or setting that genuinely distinguishes the paper;
- necessary scope boundary;
- content that must not enter the title.
- `primary_identity_claim_ids`: claims defining the first-order paper;
- `secondary_claim_ids`: valid but non-defining findings;
- `excluded_title_claim_ids`: robustness, exploratory, mechanism-support, future-only, or otherwise ineligible claims.

Freeze the primary identity before generating. Diversity may change architecture, emphasis, rhythm, design visibility, or scope visibility; it may not replace the spine with a lower-priority claim.

## 2. Choose licensed information structures

Generate only from structures the paper supports:

1. concise topic or concept;
2. topic plus central relationship or outcome domain;
3. contrast or tension;
4. conceptual hook plus precise subtitle;
5. topic plus design or substantive setting;
6. model, measurement, or theory–data task;
7. result-oriented title;
8. question title.

The last two are conditional. In the ten-paper sample, no title is a question and none reports a numeric or directional result. Retain these forms as legitimate guide-derived options, but give them low default weight and require a strong factual case.

Do not force a colon or a candidate from every family. Sample frequency is calibration, not a template.

## 3. Generate breadth before ranking

Create 8–12 working candidates across at least three genuinely applicable structures. Vary which information carries identity, not merely synonyms or word order.

Examples of meaningful variation:

- one candidate centers the object;
- one centers the paper's core contrast;
- one makes the measurement or design identity visible;
- one uses a grounded concept plus precise subtitle.

Reject generic, misleading, repetitive, or overlong candidates before presenting the shortlist.

For each candidate, remove the phrase tied to its secondary result. If no first-order paper identity remains, reject it. Diversity from promoting a secondary finding is false diversity.

## 4. Promise audit

For each content word or phrase, answer:

- What manuscript evidence licenses it?
- Is it central or merely interesting?
- Does it imply causality, a mechanism, a scope, or a contribution?
- Would a knowledgeable reader feel misled after reading the paper?

Special tests:

### `Evidence from`

Delete the phrase. If no identification, substantive-case, or scope information is lost, keep it deleted. If the following phrase names random assignment, a distinctive institution, or a defining industry or historical setting, it may earn its space.

### Colon

The first half should supply a memorable concept, wide question, or tension. The second should narrow by task, comparison, design, object, or scope. Reject when the halves repeat each other.

### Hook

The hook should be naturally tied to the paper's object or history. Reject a pun or mystery that could headline unrelated research.

### Causal term

Replace `impact` or `effect` with a neutral construction if the design does not support causation for the advertised scope.

Apply truth and centrality gates separately to every title shown to the user. A rationale admitting that a title is narrower or more secondary does not cure the failure; remove it.

## 5. Ranking rubric

Rank survivors in this order:

1. **Truth and scope fidelity** — no false promise.
2. **Central fit** — names what the paper is principally about.
3. **Distinctiveness** — differentiates the paper for the relevant audience and search context.
4. **Information density** — each word earns its space.
5. **Natural rhythm and memorability** — reads as a professional title, not keywords.
6. **Length** — concise after the earlier goals are met.

Do not average away a factual failure. A false but memorable title is rejected.

## 6. Elastic length

Use the guide's roughly 10–12-word target as a strong compression heuristic.

- about 4–8 words can work for a distinctive topic or concept title;
- about 6–14 words is a common practical range;
- about 8–14 words often supports a useful colon structure;
- about 15–18 words can be justified when a core comparison, mechanism, outcome domain, or scope boundary would otherwise disappear.

The ten calibration titles span about 5–17 words with a median near 10. This observed range supports exceptions; it does not redefine 17 words as a target.

Journal rules prevail. Never shorten by deleting the word that prevents a false universal claim or preserves the paper's defining comparison.

## 7. Final shortlist and user experience

Unless the user requests otherwise:

1. recommend one title;
2. provide two to four strong alternatives with distinct information structures;
3. briefly explain the main tradeoff in the user's language.

Do not show all 8–12 working candidates by default. The larger set is an internal exploration used to produce a selective final list.

If the current title is already strong, say so and preserve its useful elements. Improvement is not synonymous with novelty.
