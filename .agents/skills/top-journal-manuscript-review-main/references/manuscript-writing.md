# Eight Manuscript-Writing Standards

## 1. One central proposition

Make every section advance the same supported claim. Do not alternate between a causal story, a measurement story, a mechanism story, and an evaluation story unless one is clearly primary and the others are evidence or implications.

## 2. One argumentative function per section and figure

Organize by what remains to be established, not by analysis chronology. Each section should:

1. state the question;
2. present the decisive evidence;
3. define the boundary;
4. make the next question necessary.

Merge paragraph-level transitions into this structure. Do not rely on repetitive phrases such as “we further show”.

## 3. Claims equal evidence

Use stable terminology for constructs and proxies. Explicitly distinguish observed behaviour, inferred traces, measured outcomes, underlying phenomena, human evaluation, and long-run impact. Use causal verbs only when the design warrants them.

## 4. Hierarchical results reporting

Report the pattern first, the most informative effect sizes second, and robustness last. Do not narrate every plotted value. Move secondary coefficients, subgroup results, alternate taxonomies, and specification inventories to tables or supplementary material.

## 5. Methods mapped to conclusions

For every conclusion, identify the measure and design supporting it. State what each metric captures, what it excludes, why it is needed, how it is validated, and which uncertainty remains. Differentiate identification, mechanism evidence, robustness, and external validation.

## 6. Discussion adds concept rather than repetition

Use the Discussion to:

1. state the conceptual contribution;
2. explain how it changes interpretation of prior work;
3. define evidentiary limits;
4. present bounded implications;
5. identify future work only where it follows from a real limitation.

Do not repeat figure-by-figure results or introduce new empirical claims.

## 7. Authority through restraint and clarity

Prefer precise propositions over grand claims. Use short sentences where a long sentence hides assumptions. Avoid inflated scope, unnecessary taxonomies, rhetorical questions, slogans, and claims about entire systems when the study examines a specific signal or population.

## 8. Reader-centred writing discipline

Treat sentence-level writing as part of evidentiary control, not as cosmetic polishing. Every sentence should reduce the reader's effort to understand the argument and should preserve the distinction between what was measured, observed, inferred, and implied.

### One proposition per sentence

Do not combine a result, mechanism, implication, and limitation in one sentence. Split overloaded sentences so the evidence and interpretation remain distinguishable.

### Put the claim in the main clause

Do not bury the principal result in a subordinate clause, parenthesis, methodological preface, or abstract sentence ending. State the result first and explain the method or qualification next.

### Keep subjects stable and specific

Distinguish consistently among the technology, observed use, inferred traces, detector labels, papers, authors, and evaluation outcomes. Do not shift from a proxy to the underlying behaviour by changing the grammatical subject.

### Keep terminology one-to-one

Use one stable term for each construct. Do not alternate among novelty, originality, creativity, disruption, and breakthrough merely to avoid repetition. Define distinctions when multiple constructs are necessary. Accurate repetition is preferable to elegant semantic drift.

### Open paragraphs with the controlling idea

Begin with the question, tension, or conclusion that gives the paragraph a function. Avoid procedural openings such as “We next conduct additional analyses.” Make clear which alternative explanation or evidentiary gap the analysis addresses.

### Report only decision-relevant numbers

Include numbers that change the reader's assessment of direction, magnitude, persistence, uncertainty, or consistency across designs. Do not reproduce all values visible in a figure or table.

### Connect through reasoning

Replace formulaic transitions such as “We further show,” “Importantly,” and “Taken together” with the actual logical relation: contrast, unresolved alternative, identification need, boundary, or consequence.

### Match every conclusion to its evidence

Use association, consistency, and causal language deliberately. Prefer “is associated with,” “is consistent with,” and “changes measured novelty” when the evidence does not support “causes,” “demonstrates the mechanism,” or “reduces scientific novelty.”

### Sentence-level audit

For each paragraph, check:

1. Can the controlling claim be underlined in one sentence?
2. Does each sentence contain only one main proposition?
3. Is the grammatical subject the entity actually measured?
4. Do repeated terms retain the same meaning?
5. Is every number necessary for the inference?
6. Does the transition state a real logical relationship?
7. Is the final sentence no stronger than the evidence presented?

## Common failure patterns

- Treating a large dataset as proof of causal validity.
- Calling multiple correlated metrics “independent validation”.
- Presenting a detector label as direct observation of tool use.
- Interpreting lower textual novelty as lower scientific contribution.
- Claiming that two signals were previously aligned without testing that baseline.
- Suggesting one observed association explains reviewer decisions.
- Repeating the same finding in the abstract, every Results section, and each Discussion paragraph.
- Using the cover letter as an expanded abstract.

## Preferred sentence logic

Use:

`observable result -> supported interpretation -> explicit boundary`

Example:

> Rewriting the same content reduced measured novelty. Expression alone can therefore alter this signal, although the experiment does not establish whether the underlying scientific contribution changed.

Avoid:

> LLMs reduce scientific creativity and make breakthroughs harder to achieve.
