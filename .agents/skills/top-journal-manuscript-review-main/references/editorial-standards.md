# Six Editorial Standards

## 1. Problem importance

Ask whether the paper addresses a central disciplinary, cross-disciplinary, or societal problem. Reject attempts to manufacture height through words such as *revolutionize*, *fundamental*, or *unprecedented*. State why the unresolved problem changes what researchers can understand, measure, decide, or do.

## 2. Conceptual advance

Identify the new idea, not merely the new correlation, dataset, or application. Require a contribution that can be expressed in one sentence. Distinguish empirical pattern, conceptual interpretation, and practical implication.

## 3. Claim-evidence match

Audit every major claim against the exact design that supports it. Check proxy validity, construct validity, external validity, causal language, alternative explanations, and whether the outcome measures the concept named in the prose.

## 4. Identification and methodological rigour

Ask what each analysis uniquely establishes. Complementary designs should remove different doubts rather than repeat one association. Evaluate comparison groups, timing, fixed effects, pre-trends, treatment definition, measurement error, sample construction, robustness, and reproducibility.

## 5. Broad significance with boundaries

Explain why the result matters beyond the immediate dataset without extending it to untested institutions, populations, mechanisms, or outcomes. State what the study changes and what it does not establish.

## 6. Narrative discipline

Require one centre of gravity across title, abstract, introduction, figures, results, discussion, supplement, and cover letter. Remove parallel stories, repeated conclusions, robustness-as-contribution, and findings that do not alter the main inference.

## Editorial decision test

Before recommending external review, answer yes to all questions:

1. Can the central advance be stated in one sentence?
2. Is the question important before considering sample size or technique?
3. Does the strongest evidence directly address the principal claim?
4. Are the main alternative explanations meaningfully reduced?
5. Is the broad implication consequential but bounded?
6. Can a non-specialist editor understand the paper's logic quickly?
