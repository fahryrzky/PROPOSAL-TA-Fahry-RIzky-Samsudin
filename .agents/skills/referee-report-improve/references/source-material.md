## 1. Before You Start: Preliminaries

### Conflict of Interest

Alert the editor **before** preparing a report if any of the following apply:
- An author is a past coauthor (past 5 years), current colleague, former student/advisor, close personal friend, or family member
- You have a financial relationship with an author
- You have current research competing with the submission
- The paper contradicts or corrects your own research
- You have had past disputes with an author

### Previous Review

If you have reviewed the paper for another journal, notify the editor immediately. Some editors prefer fresh perspectives; others value continuity. Let the editor decide.

### Anonymity

If you cannot complete the report anonymously (e.g., you previously discussed the paper with the author and your views would be identifiable), alert the editor. Options:
- Decline to referee
- Provide advice to the editor in a private letter without a formal report
- Proceed with the report if you don't mind losing anonymity

### Declining the Invitation

- **Decide immediately** whether you can complete the review on time
- If not, decline promptly with thoughtful suggestions for alternative reviewers
- It is far better to decline upfront than to go silent or submit a very late report
- If the paper is far below the bar, a short (one-page) report is acceptable — and return it quickly (within a week) rather than spending time on lengthy analysis

### Fit Assessment

If you know little about the relevant literature, contact the editor to verify you're the right match. The editor may have had a good reason — e.g., in a paper with both theory and empirics, the editor might specifically want your expertise on one component.

## 2. Assessing Importance

### Why It Matters

The importance assessment is perhaps the hardest part of a referee's job. As one former editor of a top journal explained, the three critical determinants of publishability are: (i) whether the paper addresses a question of sufficiently broad interest, (ii) whether it makes a sufficient leap over existing literature, and (iii) whether the analysis is correct. Since most submitted papers are "correct," the decision often comes down to the first two criteria.

### The Ambition Premium

Papers that attack larger issues are more likely to have loose ends. This is not a reason to reject them. Arrow (1995) warned that "the publication selection procedure at the major journals has become methodologically more conservative, more given to preferring small wrinkles in existing analysis to genuinely new ideas."

The key question: **"Flaws and all, would I have been pleased to have written such a paper?"** If yes, that's a strong signal it deserves serious consideration for publication.

### The Innovation Option Value

Harvey and Hirshleifer (2020) formalize this insight: consider a paper with a 50% chance of being right. If it's an incremental twist, it may not be worth publishing — the upside is limited. But if it would shift the paradigm for understanding the field, this is an excellent bet. High-variance ideas have greater option value. The review process should not systematically disadvantage them.

### What the Editor Needs

A referee report should contain a **scientifically based argument** explaining the assessment of importance. Only arguments that are scientifically grounded are useful to the editor. "I don't think this is important" without reasoning is uninformative.

## 3. Structuring the Report

### First-Round Report: Three-Part Structure

**Part 1: Summary and Importance**
- Brief paragraph summarizing the paper, accessible to a non-specialist
- Discussion of the importance of the idea — broad interest, leap over existing literature, novelty

**Part 2: Essential Issues (Must Address for Publication)**
- If recommending **Rejection**: Scientific justification for why the paper is unpublishable. Not "I don't believe the proof/result." Not "This is inconsistent with so-and-so's evidence, so it must be wrong." These require concrete, evidence-based arguments.
- If recommending **R&R**: Clear, scientific explanation of each critical problem. Suggested changes and robustness checks, avoiding "make-work" for authors. No hostage-taking.

**Part 3: Suggestions (Optional for Authors)**
- Improvements that do not affect the publication decision
- Authors cannot be penalized for ignoring these
- These don't require the same rigorous justification as essential issues

### Why This Structure Matters

This structure is not cosmetic — it serves a critical function. The editor needs to know which problems are dealbreakers and which are nice-to-haves. Without clear separation, the editor — who may not be a specialist — cannot efficiently make a decision. Furthermore, mixing essential and optional comments together is inherently coercive: few authors have the luxury of risking rejection at a top journal for the sake of skipping one suggested improvement.

## 4. The Cover Letter

The cover letter should contain:

1. **Clear recommendation**: Accept / R&R / Reject. Be decisive.
2. **Brief contribution assessment**: One sentence on the paper's broad importance relative to existing work.
3. **Analysis credibility**: One sentence on whether the analysis is convincing.
4. **Publishability verdict**: Is the paper publishable as-is, likely publishable with revision, or not?
5. **Special circumstances** (if any): Evidence of unethical behavior (facts only, not emotions).

**Critical rules:**
- Keep it brief — do NOT repeat the report
- The recommendation MUST be consistent with the report
- If uncertain, suggest a second opinion with specific names and what issues they could address — but don't use this as cover for indecisiveness

## 5. Essential Issues vs. Suggestions

### The Highest-Cost Mistake

The most common and most crucial mistake referees make is failing to clearly distinguish between required changes and optional suggestions. This happens partly because of "signal-jamming" — referees feel pressure to demonstrate their intelligence to editors by inflating minor blemishes into major flaws.

### How to Decide: Essential vs. Suggestion

**Essential (Part 2) requires rigorous justification:**
- A scientifically convincing argument for why the problem renders the paper unpublishable
- The argument must be clear and understandable to the editor and authors
- The fix must be feasible within one round of revision

**Suggestion (Part 3) requires less justification:**
- These are improvements that would make the paper better but don't affect publishability
- If the author ignores a suggestion, you cannot use that as a basis for rejection
- Reasonable people can disagree about these

### On "Smell Tests" and Hunches

A referee hunch that results are wrong is **not** a scholarly argument. Specific examples of insufficient reasoning:

- **Theory paper**: "I do not believe the proof" — insufficient. You need to find a specific error in the proof or a counterexample. And if the authors demonstrate an error in your counterexample, their proposition stands. You cannot retreat into generalized skepticism.
- **Empirical paper**: "The results do not pass the smell test" — insufficient. You need to ask specific questions or request specific robustness tests. "Smell" is not a reason for rejection.

Surprising results deserve more scrutiny but not dismissal. The appropriate response is to request confirming evidence, evaluate existing robustness checks, and consider whether a limited number of additional checks would help. Then preserve the essential/suggestion boundary.

### What "Make-Work" Looks Like

**Example**: A referee insists on extending hand-collected data from 19 to 20 years. The 20th year is not special and collecting it requires visiting physical archives. The extra year would unlikely influence results. This is punitive make-work.

The test: **Does the benefit of the requested change exceed the cost to the author?** The author bears the cost, so it's easy for referees to make absurd demands thoughtlessly. Weigh the costs.

## 6. The R&R Implicit Contract

When you recommend R&R, you are making three statements:
1. The paper is important enough for the journal
2. There are problems that make it unpublishable in current form
3. These problems are correctable

This is an implicit contract: **if the author satisfactorily addresses the essential issues, you will recommend publication.**

### What This Means in Practice

- Do not invent new essential demands in round two
- Do not add items you could have reasonably asked for in round one (if you must, admit the oversight)
- If the paper is already publishable, put all additional improvements in the Suggestions section — do not hold the paper hostage
- The author's name goes on the paper, not yours — they get to decide how best to write it

### The Net Present Value Principle

In making requests, weigh the costs. It is not enough that a particular request will improve the paper. The benefits must exceed the costs, so that the improvement has positive net present value.

## 7. The Union vs. Intersection Heuristic

### The Problem with Multiple Evaluators

When there are multiple referees (plus editors/coeditors), editors often fall into the **Union Heuristic**: requiring authors to perform *all* requested tests and extensions from *all* evaluators. This is easy for the editor but dysfunctional:

- Creates bloated papers and slow publication times
- Wastes effort on excessive robustness checks
- Places innovative papers at a disadvantage (they generate more objections)
- Creates perverse incentives for signal-jamming

The polar opposite — the **Intersection Heuristic** — requires only changes suggested by *all* evaluators. Neither is optimal, but the Intersection Heuristic is a useful reference point: it reminds us that evaluator commonalities are much more likely to be important than idiosyncratic requests.

### The Up or Out Norm

Harvey and Hirshleifer (2020) propose: conditional on R&R, **90% of papers should be accepted on the first revision**. The editor should provide a clear roadmap to the author. If the authors address the essential issues, the paper is accepted.

**What this means for referees:**
- Focus on a few truly important suggestions, not a laundry list
- Referees who provide focused, high-quality reports are more valuable than those who generate voluminous lists of minor concerns
- Help the editor give a clear roadmap by being precise about what's essential

### Caveats on Shared Requests

Even when multiple referees request the same thing, it's not automatically essential. Faddish techniques, reflexive endogeneity concerns, and other common referee tropes can produce overlapping but low-value requests.

## 8. Tone, Courtesy, and Ethics

### Professional Tone

- Be courteous and focus on substance
- Avoid ascribing bad intent ("The authors were trying for a cheap publication")
- Do not insult the authors or use overly emotional language
- Reports that are "totally negative and nasty in language undercut the critical points made"
- If you must be harsh, do it in the cover letter to the editor, not in the report

### Avoiding Bias

- Consciously avoid favoring/opposing papers based on whether they confirm your existing beliefs or your own papers
- Do not unduly favor papers by well-known authors, members of your social network, or papers that cite you
- Approach the paper looking for its strengths, not just confirming your priors

### Ethical Boundaries

- Do not manipulate recommendations to advance or hold back publication of competing work
- Do not focus on adding citations to your own work
- Do not delay the reviewing process to buy time for your own related research
- If you get an idea from reading a submission that is not publicly distributed, you cannot write your own paper on the topic without referencing the submitter's prior work
- If the paper is public, you may work on the same topic but must cite it and inform the editor
- If you observe author misconduct (simultaneous submission, plagiarism), notify the editor with facts, not outrage

## 9. Second-Round Reports

### Goals

- Ideally, recommend acceptance or rejection in this round
- Omit the importance assessment (already established)
- Do NOT add items that could have been raised in round one

### When New Issues Arise

If you must raise a new essential issue:
1. Explain that you made a mistake in the first report by not catching this
2. Provide a clear scientific justification
3. This should be rare

### Avoiding Goalpost-Shifting

The worst second-round behavior: producing a new, long list of additional comments, each of which could have been detailed in the initial report. This is a breach of the implicit contract and wastes everyone's time.

## 10. Length and Formatting

### Length Guidelines

- 2–3 pages is sufficient for most reports
- Longer than 3 pages should be reserved for papers requiring extremely technical points
- Going beyond 5 pages raises the likelihood of coercion and overburdening
- A 10-page report is viewed as punitive by editors

### Formatting Requirements

- **Number all comments**, with separate numbering in each section (Part 2 and Part 3)
- No long discursive paragraphs — each point should have a clear bottom line
- The report is not a mind-dump about the paper; polish and condense

### Rejection Reports

If the paper is far below the bar, a one-page report is perfectly acceptable. Return it quickly — impress the editor by speed, not by length. But if you can't provide a straightforward scientific justification for why it's far below the bar, reconsider your assessment.

## 11. Common Anti-Patterns with Examples

### Signal-Jamming
*What it looks like:* A referee writes a lengthy report full of minor issues, each presented as though it undermines the paper's core contribution. The real motivation is demonstrating intelligence to the editor.
*Why it's harmful:* Wastes author time, bloats papers, disadvantages innovative work.
*What to do instead:* Be honest about which issues matter and which are cosmetic.

### Hostage-Taking
*What it looks like:* The paper is already publishable, but the referee recommends R&R with a long list of "essential" changes that are really just improvements.
*Why it's harmful:* Delays publication, frustrates authors and editors.
*What to do instead:* Recommend acceptance. Put improvements in the Suggestions section.

### Moving the Goalposts
*What it looks like:* In round two, the referee introduces entirely new essential demands that could have been raised in round one.
*Example:* A referee provides a thoughtful report leading to R&R. The author addresses all comments and resubmits. On the revision, the referee produces a new, long list of additional comments.
*What to do instead:* Honor the implicit contract. If the author addressed your round-one concerns, recommend acceptance.

### The Smell Test
*What it looks like:* Rejecting an empirical paper because "the results do not pass the smell test" without specifying what test should be run or what evidence would be convincing.
*What to do instead:* Request specific robustness tests. Explain what would change your mind.

### Self-Citation Inflation
*What it looks like:* A referee recommends R&R conditional on adding six additional citations to the referee's own work (beyond the two already present).
*What to do instead:* Recommend citations only when genuinely relevant to the paper's argument.

### Punitive Make-Work
*What it looks like:* Demanding that hand-collected data spanning 19 years be extended to 20 years, where the 20th year is not special and requires visiting physical archives.
*What to do instead:* Apply the NPV test — does the benefit exceed the cost to the author?

### Changing the Recommendation
*What it looks like:* A referee recommends R&R in round one. After the authors address all concerns, the referee recommends rejection in round two for "insufficient incremental contribution" — an issue that had nothing to do with the author responses and should have been raised in round one.
*What to do instead:* If there was insufficient incremental contribution, that case must be made in the initial round.
