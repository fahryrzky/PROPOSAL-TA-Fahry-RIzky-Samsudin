# Example Posts — Annotated

Four real posts generated from one finance paper, annotated to show what makes each match its platform's register. Use these to calibrate tone, length, and structural moves.

**Source paper:** *Consumers' Reaction to Corporate ESG Performance: Evidence from Store Visits* (Duan, Li, Michaely) — accepted at Management Science 2026-08-13. ESG/consumer-behavior paper using SafeGraph GPS foot-traffic data and RepRisk ESG incidents.

---

## LinkedIn

### Structural moves

1. **Hook in the first line** — three short fragments ("Three years. 11 million store-months. One stubborn question.") that telegraph scale and stakes.
2. **Frame the debate** — "or is the whole ESG discussion just press releases and investor theater?" sets up the contribution.
3. **Data scale callout** — the two datasets named explicitly with numbers (6M+ stores, 80,000 sources, 20 languages).
4. **Numbered findings** using 1️⃣ 2️⃣ 3️⃣ 4️⃣ 5️⃣ glyphs — LinkedIn convention for business posts.
5. **Identification paragraph** — explains the temperature-shock design in plain English, then states what it rules out.
6. **Business takeaway** — "What it means for business leaders: …"
7. **Paper credit** with verified affiliations, SSRN link rendered as markdown link.
8. **5 hashtags** at the bottom.

### Example excerpt

```
Three years. 11 million store-months. One stubborn question.

Do consumers actually punish firms for ESG scandals, or is the whole ESG discussion just press releases and investor theater?

My co-authors Tinghua Duan, Roni Michaely, and I built a dataset to find out. We combined two sources finance had never merged before:

→ SafeGraph: GPS-derived daily foot traffic for 6M+ U.S. points of interest
→ RepRisk: verified ESG incidents (pollution, labor abuses, governance failures) from 80,000 media and stakeholder sources in 20 languages

266 publicly listed firms. January 2018 to September 2020.

The findings:

1️⃣ One ESG incident in a month = roughly a 1.2% drop in store visits the next month. The hit lasts the full year. No rebound.
...
```

### Why it works on LinkedIn

- Evidence-led — every claim has a number behind it.
- Business-framed — practitioners can extract a takeaway without reading the paper.
- Visually scannable — short paragraphs, numbered glyphs, arrows as bullets.
- No "I'm excited to share" opener. The hook earns the read.

## X / Twitter (5-tweet thread)

### Structural moves

1. **`1/` hook** — the question + the causal claim + the data scale in one tweet.
2. **`2/` headline result** — magnitude + the asymmetry that prevents the naive read (E/S yes, governance no).
3. **`3/` heterogeneity** — bullets of where the effect concentrates.
4. **`4/` causality** — the temperature-shock design, ending with what it rules out.
5. **`5/` bottom line** — real-world implication + paper credit + SSRN link.

### Example excerpt

```
1/ Do consumers actually punish firms for ESG scandals?

Our new paper says yes — and we can show it's causal.

11.4M store-months of GPS foot traffic (SafeGraph) × verified ESG incidents (RepRisk). 266 publicly listed US firms. 2018–2020.

2/ Headline result

One ESG incident in a given month → ~1.2% drop in store visits the next month. The effect is durable — no rebound over the following 12 months.

Drop is concentrated in environmental and social incidents. Governance scandals barely register. Consumers are reacting to values, not "bad firm" signals.
...
```

### Why it works on X

- Each tweet is self-contained but the arc builds.
- Terse sentences, contrarian-friendly opener.
- Zero hashtags (finance-academic Twitter convention — LinkedIn is the hashtag platform, X is not).
- Identification explained in two short paragraphs, not a methods section.
- The thread is the unit; a reader who sees only tweet 4 still gets a complete thought.

### Single-tweet alternative (~280 chars)

```
Do consumers actually punish ESG offenders?

New paper: yes — and we can show it's causal.

11M store-months of GPS foot traffic (SafeGraph) × RepRisk incidents. One ESG event ≈ 1.2% drop in next-month visits. Effect amplified by heat waves, only for environmental incidents.

Accepted, Management Science.
https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4584361
```

## 小红书 (Xiaohongshu)

### Structural moves

1. **First-person research opener** — "今天分享一项我们团队近期完成的研究 🔬" — no "姐妹们/家人们".
2. **The research question as a bold callout** with 👉 emoji marker.
3. **📌 section dividers** — 研究方法, 主要发现, 因果识别策略, 最重要的结论, 论文发表信息.
4. **Numbered findings ①②③④** inside 主要发现 — not prose paragraphs.
5. **Academic vocabulary** — 因果识别, 外生冲击, 替代解释, 非对称性.
6. **Publication status** — "本研究已被 Management Science 接收，即将刊出 ✨".
7. **6–8 hashtags** mixing Chinese and English research tags.

### Example excerpt

```
今天分享一项我们团队近期完成的研究 🔬

研究持续三年，围绕一个核心问题展开：
👉 **当一家企业发生 ESG 负面事件，消费者是否会以实际行动做出反应？**

答案是肯定的——并且这一反应具有因果性 💡

📌 **数据与方法**

本研究合并了两组此前从未被结合使用的数据：

📊 SafeGraph：覆盖全美 600 万个商业网点的匿名 GPS 人流数据
📰 RepRisk：基于 80,000 个媒体与利益相关方来源的 ESG 事件记录
...
📌 **因果识别策略**

这是本研究难度最高的部分。我们采用"异常高温"作为外生冲击——炎热天气会提升居民对气候议题的关注度，但与企业自身经营状况无关。

实证结果显示：
• 在刚刚经历异常高温的县（county），消费者对 ESG 事件的负面反应显著更强
• 这种放大效应仅出现在环境类事件上，对社会与治理类事件不成立

该非对称性排除了"丑闻与客流下降同源于公司整体经营恶化"等替代解释。
```

### Why it works on 小红书 (for an academic account)

- Professional register — no lifestyle-influencer markers that would mismatch a researcher's account.
- Card-style structure with 📌 dividers — native to the platform.
- Emojis used as visual structure, not emotional decoration.
- Research vocabulary signals expertise without being impenetrable.
- Publication status gives the post credibility weight.

### What was changed from the first draft (learning example)

The first draft opened with "姐妹们！今天不是种草，是分享一个我们团队刚刚做完的研究" and used "灵魂问题", "巨", "真的", "粉转黑/黑转粉". The user asked for professional academic tone. The rewrite removed all lifestyle markers and added academic vocabulary (因果识别策略, 外生冲击, 替代解释, 非对称性). The card-style structure stayed — that is platform convention, separate from voice.

## Substack

### Structural moves

1. **Title as question** — "Does ESG actually change what people buy?"
2. **Italicized subtitle** — one-sentence framing of the contribution.
3. **Open with the puzzle** — why was this question hard to answer for decades? (Alternative: a clearly-hypothetical illustrative scene.)
4. **Data breakthrough paragraph** — the two datasets, named and characterized.
5. **Findings in narrative order** — headline → heterogeneity → spillovers → causality → firm response. Each as a short paragraph or bolded mini-section.
6. **Causality gets its own `## section`** — because it is the hardest part and the reader will want to follow the logic.
7. **Closing reflection** — what the paper suggests about the world beyond the specific finding.
8. **Italicized footer** — paper credit, affiliations, data sources, SSRN link, contact email.

### Example excerpt (opening + closing)

```
# Does ESG actually change what people buy?

*A new paper uses 11 million store-months of American foot traffic to find out whether consumers really punish companies for bad behavior. The answer is yes — and the data tells a more interesting story than either ESG advocates or skeptics usually admit.*

---

For decades, finance economists have argued about whether corporate ESG behavior actually changes what people buy. The intuition is straightforward. If consumers care about sustainability, they should punish firms that pollute, exploit workers, or commit governance fraud, and reward firms that don't. In aggregate, those choices should show up in sales and stock prices.

The evidence, though, was thin. Quarterly sales data are too coarse to see real-time reactions to specific events. ESG ratings from different providers disagree with each other so often that academics now treat them as almost uninterpretable. And separating "people stopped shopping because of the scandal" from "people stopped shopping because the company is generally bad" has been nearly impossible with the data we had.
...
```

```
What it does suggest is that the boring, mundane act of choosing which store to walk into is, in aggregate, a form of corporate governance. Every green purchase, every boycott, every "I'm not going back there" is a small vote cast against the version of the company that did the bad thing.

---

*Accepted at Management Science. Working paper with Tinghua Duan (EDHEC Business School), Frank Weikai Li (City University of Hong Kong), and Roni Michaely (University of Hong Kong and ECGI). Data: SafeGraph and RepRisk.*

📄 *Read the full paper on SSRN:* [https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4584361](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4584361)
```

### Why it works on Substack

- Long-form essay register, not a press release.
- Earns the reader's attention with the puzzle before the finding.
- Causality section explains the *logic* of the identification, not just the result.
- Closing reflection lands the bigger meaning — consumer choice as corporate governance — rather than restating the coefficient.
- No emojis, no hashtags. Substack is text.

## Cross-platform pattern

The same paper yields four genuinely different artifacts. The through-line (puzzle → data → finding → identification → implication) is constant; the voice, length, structure, and visual register change per platform. That is the skill: not four translations of one post, but four posts calibrated to four reading postures.
